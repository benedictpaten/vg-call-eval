# Plan: `vg-call-eval` — a standalone concordance and performance harness

Implements stages **3b**, **4** and **4b** of
[vg-read-likelihood-design.md](vg-read-likelihood-design.md), outside the vg tree so that none of
its dependencies (`aardvark`, `truvari`, Python analysis stack) land in vg's build.

Lives at **[github.com/benedictpaten/vg-call-eval](https://github.com/benedictpaten/vg-call-eval)**
(public). Tier 0 (simulation) and tier 2 (real HG002 data) both run; the generated results tables are
in that repo's `docs/`, not here, because they are regenerated from run artefacts rather than
transcribed. §6 records what remains.

**How to read this document.** "Where this stands" below is the standing summary and the only part
maintained as things change. §0–§8 are the harness design. **Appendix A is a chronological
investigation log** — go to it through its index, for the derivation behind a number, not front to
back. **Appendix B lists the claims made here and later withdrawn**, and is worth checking before
quoting anything from the log.

## Where this stands

Everything below §0 is the harness design; **Appendix A is a chronological investigation log** and
should be read through its index rather than front to back. This section is the standing summary, and
is the only part that is maintained as things change.

### Measured

Two chromosomes (chr20, chr6) × two graphs (4- and 34-haplotype) × five arms, HG002 against the GIAB
defrabb V0.019 draft benchmark on CHM13v2.0, all re-run on one build. Small variants by aardvark,
structural variants by truvari. Tables live in the harness repo's `docs/`, regenerated by
`report.py --contig` and `compare_graphs.py --contig`.

**Genotype F1** — the read-likelihood caller leads everywhere, and its margin widens with graph
richness:

| | chr20 4-hap | chr20 34-hap | chr6 4-hap | chr6 34-hap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson-z` | 0.9359 | 0.9124 | 0.9466 | 0.9318 |
| `readlik-z` | **0.9490** | **0.9547** | **0.9586** | **0.9616** |

**Structural variants (truvari)** — the weak class, and the deficit is real rather than metric
artefact:

| | chr20 4-hap | chr20 34-hap | chr6 4-hap | chr6 34-hap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson-z` | **0.4930** | 0.4391 | **0.5478** | **0.4881** |
| `readlik-z` | 0.4824 | **0.4437** | 0.5349 | 0.4724 |

**Cost** — no longer an argument against the model. On chr20 `readlik-z` runs 99 s against
`poisson-z`'s 75 s, and `readlik` is *faster* than `poisson` (120 s vs 168 s).

### Settled, and acted on

1. **`--mismap-max` 0.1 → 0.5** (§9.20). The old cap was overriding the mapper on haplotype-rich
   graphs. A clamp that is inert on a sparse graph is not thereby harmless.
2. **`--mismap-min` → 0.02** (§9.21), re-swept at the corrected cap and scored on *both* benchmarks.
3. **`AD`, `BL`, `GQI` emitted; `GQ` scaled by `sum(AD)/DP`** (§9.26–9.27). Changes no genotype;
   removes 44–58% of false small-variant calls at unchanged recall.
4. **The read path is 5.8× faster with byte-identical output** (§9.17).
5. **truvari is the SV metric**, not aardvark's `Sv*` categories (§9.22).

### Settled, and deliberately not acted on

- **No binomial coverage-skew term.** The length-aware prior it needs is contradicted by direct
  measurement — true het ALT share is 0.50 in every length class from 0 bp to over 1 kb — and the
  flat-null form is the weakest of five signals everywhere (§9.26).
- **No minimum depth filter.** Dominated by lowering the GQ threshold to the same recall, in all
  eight dataset × benchmark cells. Low depth already depresses GQ (§9.27).
- **No maximum depth filter as a default.** It passes that test in one configuration only (§9.27).
- **No hard pile-up guard.** It beats *no* filter, not the ranking already in the file (§9.26).

### Known limits of what has been established

- **One sample, one read type.** HG002, 151 bp Illumina, two chromosomes. Every default set here was
  fitted on that.
- **SV n is small.** ~370–965 labelled calls per dataset. Single-feature conclusions hold at that n;
  multi-feature fits do not.
- **The benchmark is a draft** and its small-variant half holds no record ≥50 bp, which is why
  anything size-restricted goes to truvari.
- **Graph and alignment move together.** Reads mapped to one graph cannot be scored against another,
  so no 4-vs-34-haplotype row is a single-variable experiment.

## 0. What the three stages need, restated as requirements

| Stage | What it must produce |
|---|---|
| **3b** | De novo genotype concordance against a truth set. vg's in-tree harness only covers the `-v` re-genotyping path, so the default `FlowCaller` path has never been measured against truth at all. |
| **4** | The read-likelihood caller vs the Poisson caller: precision/recall/F1 stratified by variant class, a `GQ` sweep, the mismap-term-off comparison, and wall-clock/memory. |
| **4b** | A `GQ` reliability curve, and a fitted scalar `read_weight` that makes it diagonal. |

One hard constraint that shapes everything: the harness must be able to **compare two vg builds**
(patched vs unpatched). So it takes a `vg` binary path *per arm*, never links or imports vg, and
records `vg version` with every result for provenance.

The first use of that facility settled a question rather than measuring a difference: patching the
`depth_err` ternary produces **byte-identical calls**, because its only consumer inside
`genotype_likelihood` is commented out and it never reaches the VCF. Earlier drafts of this plan
and of the design doc claimed that bug made the Poisson caller a mis-parameterised baseline and
gated stage 4. **That was wrong.** The baseline is fair exactly as shipped.

## 1. The data problem, which is worse than it looks

**Do not build this on the in-tree HGSVC fixture.** I checked what its truth VCF actually contains:

```
17 variants, all SVs >= 50bp, of which only 8 are non-reference in HG00514
```

Eight true positives. That cannot support a precision/recall estimate, let alone the binned
reliability curve stage 4b needs — which wants hundreds of calls per `GQ` bin. It is a useful
*regression smoke test* and nothing more, and the plan should say so rather than quietly produce
three-significant-figure F1 scores from eight variants.

So the harness is built in **three tiers**, and the stages are satisfied at different tiers:

| Tier | Data | Truth quality | Runtime | Satisfies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | Simulated from a small graph with known haplotypes (`vg sim -a`) | **Exact**, for genotypes *and* per-read origin | seconds–minutes | 3b, 4, 4b (primary) |
| **1** | vg's HGSVC chr22 fixture, real reads | 8 non-ref SVs | ~1 min | regression smoke test only |
| **2** | HG002 on HPRC v2.1 MC CHM13, GIAB draft benchmark, confident regions | Real, community standard (*draft*) | hours per chromosome | 3b, 4 (credible external numbers) — **specified, see §9** |

**Tier 0 is the backbone, and that is a feature, not a compromise.** Simulation is the only tier that
gives per-read origin truth, which is what the §4.4 MAPQ escape route needs, and the only one where
depth, read length, error rate and ploidy can be dialled to probe a specific regime. Its weakness is
equally clear and must be stated in the README: simulated reads are generated from the graph, so
mapping is unrealistically easy and absolute precision/recall will be optimistic. **Tier 0 is for
comparing callers to each other and for calibration; tier 2 is for absolute numbers.**

Tier 2 is opt-in and downloads on demand. Nothing large is committed to the repo.

### 1.1 Tier 0 must be tuned to discriminate, or it measures nothing

Found on the first real runs, and it changes how tier-0 configs should be written. At 20 kb and 20x
with 150 bp reads, **every arm scores F1 = 1.0000** — the callers are genotyping variants that are in
the graph, from reads simulated off those exact haplotypes and mapped back to the same graph. There is
no ambiguity for a likelihood model to resolve better than a count model, so the comparison is empty.

At 4x with 100 bp reads the same harness separates them. Low depth and short reads are what create the
ambiguity the model is supposed to handle well, so **the default tier-0 config is a hard one**
(`configs/tier0-hard.yaml`), not a comfortable one.

The general rule, worth stating because a perfect score is easy to misread as good news: **if a
configuration gives every arm a perfect score, that is a fact about the configuration, not about the
callers.** The harness README says so too.

**Tier 2 is deferred until tiers 0 and 1 are working. The read-source blocker is now cleared.**
`InMemorySiteReadSource` holds every read in memory, which is fine for a simulated contig or a 760 kb
region and hopeless for a whole genome, so stage 6 of the design doc was a prerequisite for tier 2 rather
than an optional optimisation. Stages 6, 6b and 7 are built and measured: on 80 k reads over 400 kb, peak
RSS falls from 309 MB to 56 MB with no change to the calls.

**Tier 2 should use `--gam-index`, not `--gaf-base`.** Both bound memory to about the same figure (56 MB
vs 52 MB), but the indexed GAM backend is roughly twice as fast and needs no external binary, whereas
gaf-base requires `gbz-base` on the `PATH`. gaf-base's advantage is disk footprint (2.8 MB against 7.6 MB
here), which is not the constraint tier 2 runs into. See §6.4 of the design doc for the measurements.

## 2. Comparison engine

Naive position-and-allele matching is not an option. vg emits variants in graph coordinates flattened
onto a reference path, and the same variant legitimately differs from the truth representation in
anchor base, left/right alignment, and multi-allelic grouping. Getting this wrong manufactures both
false positives and false negatives.

### 2.1 Primary engine: `aardvark`

**Use [aardvark](https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/aardvark) (PacBio, MIT, single Rust binary) as
the primary engine.** It replaces what this plan originally split across `rtg vcfeval` and
`truvari bench`, and it suits this project better than either.

How it works: it loads variants within user-supplied BED regions, splits those regions further wherever
consecutive variants are more than `--min-variant-gap` apart, and then within each sub-region searches
for **the set of phased variant zygosities minimising edit distance between the truth and query
haplotype sequences**. Truth phasing is honoured; query phasing is deliberately ignored so phasing
errors are not counted as genotype errors. That is the same haplotype-replay idea that makes `vcfeval`
correct about representation differences, generalised to any variant size.

Four reasons it is the better choice here:

1. **One engine for every size class.** Variant-type agnostic by construction, with native
   stratification into SNV / indel / SV / tandem repeat in `summary.tsv`. That removes the arbitrary
   50 bp boundary and, more importantly, removes the need to reconcile two tools' different result
   schemas and matching semantics. SVs need `--min-variant-gap 1000`; tandem repeats additionally
   want `--enable-record-basepair`.
2. **It removes a dependency problem rather than adding one.** MIT licence, single Rust binary, no
   Java, no Docker, and no licence-acceptance step — the awkward part of `rtg-tools` in CI.
   ⚠ **Correction from building it:** aardvark publishes *only* an `x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu`
   release asset — no macOS, no ARM. It is pure Rust (`noodles`, not `rust-htslib`) so it builds
   cleanly from source in a few minutes, verified on macOS arm64 with v0.10.5, but "download a binary"
   is only true on Linux x86_64 and CI on other platforms must build it.
3. **Native `--stratifications` by BED**, which is code this plan would otherwise have had to write.
4. **Partial credit, which matters unusually much for *this* caller** — see §2.2.

Two details found on reading the actual CLI, both of which simplify the plan. `--truth-sample` /
`--query-sample` select the sample directly, so **no `bcftools` renaming step is needed**. And
`--max-branch-factor` (default 50) explicitly "limits work on dense variant regions" — a built-in
guard on exactly the tractability risk in §8, which downgrades it from unmitigated to bounded.

### 2.2 Why BASEPAIR partial credit is the right instrument here

Aardvark offers three metric families. The distinction is not cosmetic for this project:

- **`GT`** — per-variant TP/FP/FN following hap.py's methodology. Binary. Zygosity mismatches are
  split asymmetrically (a het call against a hom truth registers as truth TP plus query FP).
- **`HAP`** — ALT allele accuracy counted per haplotype.
- **`BASEPAIR`** — sequence-level edit distance, so a variant is weighted by its length (an indel of
  length L counts L, an SNV counts 1) and **bases can be scored fractionally** (TP=0.5, FN=0.5). All
  reported values are doubled to keep the arithmetic integral, so a reported TP of 5 means 2.5 bases.

The read-likelihood caller's expected advantage is concentrated exactly where binary metrics are least
sensitive: repeat-rich and SV-adjacent sites, where it may resolve an allele *better* — a breakpoint a
few bases closer, a long allele more nearly right — without flipping any TP/FP decision. A binary
metric scores that as no change. `BASEPAIR` measures how close the called haplotype sequence actually
is, so it can detect the model improving allele resolution before that improvement is large enough to
change a call. **Report `GT` for comparability with what reviewers expect, and `BASEPAIR` as the
sensitive instrument.** Where they disagree, that disagreement is itself a finding.

### 2.3 Retain `truvari` as an SV cross-check

**Status: SVs are now scored with aardvark (§9.11), so truvari is no longer the only route to an SV
number — but the case for it as a second opinion got stronger, not weaker.** Aardvark turned out to
leave `query_total`/`query_tp`/`query_fp` at zero for its `Sv*` categories, so its own SV precision and
F1 are unusable and had to be recomputed from per-variant decisions. That is exactly the class of
single-tool quirk an independent engine would catch.

Not as a primary engine, but on at least one dataset, for two reasons. Aardvark's `performance.md` is
an explicit placeholder — no documented limits, failure modes, or tractability bounds — and truvari
remains the community reference for SV benchmarking specifically. An independent second opinion is cheap
insurance against a systematic quirk in a single tool.

`rtg vcfeval` is dropped. Aardvark's `GT` metric covers the same ground with hap.py-compatible
semantics and none of the licence friction.

Engines sit behind one internal interface returning a common per-variant record, so adding `hap.py`
later touches no analysis code.

**Pre-comparison normalisation** is its own step and must be explicit, because silent mistakes here
look like caller regressions: `bcftools norm -f <ref> -m -any` to split multi-allelics and left-align;
restrict to the evaluation region; reconcile sample names (vg emits `SAMPLE` by default, HGSVC truth is
`HG00514`); and require a **confident-region BED**, without which false negatives outside callable
territory are meaningless.

## 3. Metrics

### 3.1 Concordance

Aardvark's `summary.tsv` already provides much of this, so the harness consumes rather than
recomputes it. `GT` precision/recall/F1 as the headline, `BASEPAIR` alongside it (§2.2), and both
stratified by:

- class: SNP, indel 1–49 bp, SV >= 50 bp
- size bin within each class
- region strata if a tier-2 BED provides them (repeats, segdups — where a graph caller should win)

### 3.2 The `GQ` sweep, which is the fair comparison

Comparing two callers at their default thresholds is misleading when one of them is known to be
over-confident (§4.6 risk 2 of the design doc). **The headline stage-4 output is a precision/recall
curve swept over `GQ`**, one line per configuration, not a single F1 number. That answers the question
that actually matters — "at matched precision, which caller has better recall?" — and is robust to the
two callers' `GQ` fields not meaning the same thing.

Single-threshold tables are still emitted, clearly labelled as a slice of the curve.

### 3.3 Performance

Wall-clock and peak RSS per configuration (`/usr/bin/time -l` on macOS, `-v` on Linux), plus vg's own
`--progress` stage timings when available. The design doc predicts read *retrieval* dominates rather
than scoring, so the harness should report the split where vg exposes it, and at minimum give total
runtime as a function of depth and region size. This is the "general performance testing" side and it
is cheap once the run matrix exists.

## 4. Stage 4b, and a simplification that makes it nearly free

The obvious reading of 4b is: fit `read_weight`, rebuild vg, re-run everything, re-measure. That is
not necessary, and seeing why is worth the paragraph.

`read_weight` multiplies the whole per-read sum, so for every genotype
`ln P(reads | G) -> w · ln P(reads | G)`. Two consequences:

1. **The calls do not change.** Scaling every genotype's log-likelihood by `w > 0` preserves their
   ordering, so argmax is invariant. `read_weight` is purely a confidence recalibration.
2. **`GQ` is exactly linear in `w`.** The implementation computes
   `GQ = phred(second) − phred(best) = (10/ln10)·(best − second)`, a difference of the scaled sums, so
   `GQ_w = w · GQ_1`.

Therefore **`w` can be fitted entirely post-hoc from a single vg run**, from the `(GQ, correct?)` pairs
the concordance step already produces. No rebuild, no re-run, and no need to re-do the concordance to
validate — only to recompute the reliability curve with `GQ` scaled.

Procedure:

1. From the comparison output, pair every called variant with its `GQ` and whether its genotype matched
   truth. **Use aardvark's `GT` decision here, not `BASEPAIR`.** `GQ` asserts a probability that the
   genotype is wrong — a binary event — so binning fractional partial credit against it would be a
   category error. `BASEPAIR` is the right instrument for §2.2 and the wrong one for calibration.
2. Bin by `GQ`. Observed error rate per bin = `1 − TP_gt / called`. Expected from the field's stated
   meaning = `10^(−GQ/10)`.
3. Report the reliability curve plus a scalar summary (expected calibration error; Brier score).
4. Grid-search `w` to minimise calibration error over bins. One-dimensional, seconds.
5. Only then does vg need a `--read-weight` flag, and only to *emit* the calibrated value.

Caveat to keep honest: `GP` does **not** scale linearly (`logsumexp` of scaled terms is not the scaled
logsumexp), so if `GP` calibration is ever wanted it needs a real re-run. Only `GQ` is free.

**Optional module, cheap because tier 0 already simulates:** MAPQ versus *true* mismapping rate, read
by read, from `vg sim -a`'s recorded origin. That is the measurement behind the design doc's 61-entry
recalibration table and the empirical answer to whether the `e_max` clamp is set sensibly. Worth having
even though the design doc defers the table itself.

## 5. Architecture

```
vg-call-eval/
  README.md                  # includes the tier-0-is-optimistic caveat, prominently
  pyproject.toml
  src/vgcalleval/
    cli.py                   # `vgcalleval run|report|calibrate|fetch`
    orchestrate.py           # run matrix, output-exists-and-newer caching
    callers.py               # vg invocation matrix; records `vg version` per run
    truth.py                 # normalisation, region/sample reconciliation
    engines/aardvark.py      # primary: all size classes, GT + BASEPAIR
    engines/truvari.py       # SV cross-check only
    engines/base.py          # common per-variant record schema
    metrics.py               # strata, GQ sweep, PR curves
    calibrate.py             # 4b: reliability, read_weight fit
    mapq.py                  # optional: MAPQ vs true mismapping
    perf.py                  # timing / peak RSS
    report.py                # markdown + PNG; single self-contained HTML
  configs/
    tier0-sim.yaml
    tier1-hgsvc.yaml
    tier2-giab.yaml
  data/fetch.sh              # tier-2 downloads; nothing large committed
  tests/                     # unit tests on synthetic VCF pairs
```

Two design points worth stating:

- **The run matrix is declarative.** A config lists vg binaries, caller configurations, and datasets;
  the harness takes the cross product. Adding "Poisson with `depth_err` fixed" was a config line, not
  code. That matters because quantifying that bug's effect on the baseline is a stage-4 deliverable.
- **Comparison output is a table, not a report.** Every engine emits one row per variant
  (`dataset, config, chrom, pos, class, size, GQ, truth_gt, called_gt, gt_decision, bp_tp, bp_fn`)
  into Parquet or TSV. Aardvark's labelled `query.vcf.gz` carries the decision plus `EA`/`OA`/`RI`; the
  documentation does not say whether it preserves `FORMAT/GQ`, so the harness joins `GQ` back from the
  original query VCF on (chrom, pos, ref, alt) rather than assuming. Every metric, curve and calibration fit is then a pure function of that table, so re-analysis
  never re-runs vg. This is what makes 4b post-hoc.

### 5.1 Orchestration: recommend plain Python first

Given the stated wish to avoid complexity, **v1 should be plain Python 3 driving subprocesses**, with
make-style staleness checks (skip if output exists and is newer than inputs). Dependencies: `bcftools`
via subprocess, `pandas`, `matplotlib`, `pyyaml`, the `aardvark` release binary, and `truvari` for the
cross-check. No Java, no Docker, no licence prompts.

Escalate only if the matrix outgrows it — the upgrade path is Snakemake (conventional, good caching) or
**Toil**, which would be the natural choice if this ever needs to run many samples on a cluster. I would
not start there: a harness that is hard to debug is worse than one that recomputes.

## 8. Risks

- **Tier 0 optimism.** Simulated reads make mapping easy, so absolute precision/recall will flatter
  both callers. Mitigation: tier 0 is only ever used for *relative* comparison and calibration, stated
  in the README and in every generated report header.
- **Normalisation bugs masquerading as caller differences.** Mitigation: M1's self-comparison test, and
  asserting that the two callers' *shared* calls compare identically against truth.
- **Calibration fitted on tier 0 may not transfer** to real data — the design doc already notes a MAPQ
  table is specific to mapper, graph, read length and error profile, and the same caution applies to
  `read_weight`. Report it as tier-specific and re-fit at tier 2.
- ~~**Aardvark on dense pangenome variation is still unverified**~~ — **closed** at tier 2 (M0). Retained below for the record — *downgraded, not closed.* It handled
  every tier-0 callset without incident, and `--max-branch-factor` (default 50) is a built-in bound on
  the optimiser in dense regions, which is more mitigation than the plan originally assumed existed.
  But tier-0 variants are deliberately spaced 20 bp apart, so this has *not* been tested at real
  pangenome density. Re-check at tier 2, and keep the engine interface pluggable.
- **A configuration that does not discriminate looks like agreement between callers.** Every arm
  scoring 1.0 means the test was too easy, not that the callers are equivalent (§1.1). Mitigation:
  default to the hard config, and treat any all-perfect result as a config bug to investigate rather
  than a result to report.
- **Single-replicate differences are noise.** The first discriminating run separated the callers by
  3 true positives out of 141. That is not a result. Configs carry a replicate seed list, and
  differences should be reported across replicates or not at all.
- **`GQ` capped at 256 in vg's output** truncates the top of the reliability curve. The harness should
  detect saturation and exclude saturated bins from the fit rather than fitting through them.

### 9.2 Two findings that change the experiment, not just its parameters

> **Resolved by the run.** (a) turned out to be *false for small variants and true for SVs* — see §9.9
> and §9.11. (b) held exactly as written. Both are left as first written, with the outcomes recorded in
> place, because the controls they motivated are what made the result interpretable.

**(a) HG002 is absent from the graph, so there is no circularity — but only 4 haplotypes are present,
which makes *allele enumeration* the likely binding constraint rather than the caller.** *(Outcome: not
for small variants — the two enumerations differ by ~0.001 F1, §9.9 — but yes for SVs, where haplotype
enumeration is worth ~0.04 F1, §9.11.)* The graph
carries CHM13, GRCh38 and two `recombination` haplotypes: this is a haplotype-sampled personalised
graph, the kind `vg giraffe` maps against, not the full 90-haplotype pangenome. With `-z`, candidate
alleles come only from those four walks, so **any true HG002 allele absent from all four cannot be
called at all**, by either caller.

This has to be controlled for, or we will report a graph limitation as a caller limitation:

- Run **both** enumeration modes — `-z` (haplotype-based, 4 walks) **and** `-k` with a pack file
  (`FlowTraversalFinder`, support-based, not restricted to those walks).
- The gap between them **is** the measurement of what the sampled graph costs. That is worth reporting
  in its own right, and it is a number we do not currently have for this graph.
- The `-k` arm needs `vg pack` over the reads, which is a real cost (§9.4).

It also means absolute recall will read low against a whole-genome benchmark. **That is expected and
must be stated in the report**, or the numbers will be misread as the caller failing.

**(b) The in-memory read source cannot be used, even for a single chromosome.** From the stage-6
measurement, held reads cost roughly 3.2 KB each. chr20 carries about 12.5 M records, so:

```
12.5 M reads x 3.2 KB  ~=  40 GB      >  32 GB laptop
```

Tier 2 therefore **requires** a windowed backend — `--gam-index` or `--gaf-base` — where resident reads
are bounded by one window rather than by the chromosome. This is the first place stages 6/7 are a hard
requirement rather than an optimisation.

### 9.3 Do *not* reach for the per-chromosome gbz-base machinery here

The graph loads in 4.3 GB and fits comfortably, so a chromosome subgraph can be cut with `vg chunk`
directly. The `gbz-base` per-contig extraction path (design doc §6.4, `call-per-contig.py`) exists for
graphs that do *not* fit; using it here would add a runtime dependency and a GBZ rebuild for no gain.

What genuinely is at risk on 32 GB is **genome-wide snarl computation**: `IntegratedSnarlFinder` runs
over the whole graph and `SnarlManager` retains every snarl, and node iteration alone already costs
8.8 GB on 100 M nodes. Rather than find out whether that fits, work per chromosome, where it is ~2% of
the problem. So: **per-chromosome for the snarls, `vg chunk` for the cutting, no gbz-base.**

### 9.5 Comparison engine: keep aardvark, but cross-check against GIAB's own recommendation

The benchmark README recommends **`hap.py --engine vcfeval`** for small variants and **`truvari bench`

the result legible to anyone comparing against their published figures.

### 9.6 Caveats to carry into the report

- **The benchmark is a draft.** Its own README: "preliminary draft benchmark sets and likely include
  errors", with known problems in "highly homozygous regions, homopolymers, and tandem repeats", and it
  explicitly excludes VDJ and the TSPY2 translocation region. Fine for caller-vs-caller comparison;
  quote this before quoting any absolute number.
- **Recall is bounded by the 4-haplotype graph**, not only by the caller (§9.2a).
- **Mates are not independent.** The reads are paired and design §4.1 treats every read as an
  independent observation. Correlated mates make genotype likelihoods overconfident by an unknown
  factor, which matters specifically for stage 4b calibration. Worth measuring: fit `read_weight` with
  both mates and with one mate per pair, and compare.
- **One chromosome is one sample of the genome.** chr20 is the community-conventional choice and is
  reasonable, but it is not representative of hard regions. chr21/chr22 are cheaper if iteration speed
  matters more than convention.

### 9.7 The reusable read artifact: one whole-genome GAF-Base

**Decision: sort the whole-genome GAF with `gaf-base sort`, build one GAF-Base, and call with
`--gaf-base`.** This replaces an earlier recommendation in this document to convert to GAM and sort with
`vg gamsort`; that recommendation was wrong, and the measurements below are why. It also removes the need
to split reads per chromosome at all — one artifact serves every chromosome.

**A sorted whole-genome GAF is not itself a useful artifact**, which is worth stating because it is the
obvious thing to reach for. `vg gamsort` will sort GAF (`-G`), but its `-i/--index` is documented under
*GAM sorting options* only: there is no `.gai` for GAF, and the tabix-GAF backend (design §6.3) was never
implemented. So a sorted GAF gives no random access on its own — extracting one chromosome from it still
means a full scan. Its only use is as input to `gaf-base construct`, which is exactly how it is used here.

#### Measured, on this data, 8 threads where applicable

Both routes were run on the **same 8 M-record sample** of the real GAF:

| route | wall | peak RSS | artifact |
|---|---|---|---|
| `vg convert -F` + `vg gamsort -i` | **273 s** | 13.3 GB | 895 MB GAM + 1 MB `.gai` |
| `gaf-base sort` + `gaf-base construct` | **88 s** | 13.2 GB | 1,493 MB db |

`gaf-base sort` alone is the striking part: **17.2 s at 0.9 GB peak, single-threaded**, against
`vg gamsort`'s 249 s at 13.3 GB with 8 threads — 14× faster on a fifteenth of the memory. Sorting is the
dominant cost in the GAM route and a rounding error in this one.

Projected to the full 588 M records, with the per-step rates measured separately:

| step | projected | **actually built, 596,017,764 alignments** |
|---|---|---|
| `gaf-base sort` | ~21 min | **47 min**, 6.5 GB peak |
| `gaf-base construct` | ~64 min, ~77 GB | **51 min**, 15.0 GB peak, db **20.74 GB** |
| `gbz-base construct` (query target) | — | **190 s**, 10.7 GB peak, **6.27 GB** |
| **total wall** | ~1.5 h | **51 min** — sort and construct overlap through the FIFO, so the total is the *longer* of the two, not their sum |
| *(GAM route, for contrast)* | | *~6 h, ~66 GB, no overlap available* |

Two things beat the projection: the FIFO turns two serial steps into one concurrent pipeline, and the
database came out 3.7× smaller than extrapolated (below).

**`gaf-base construct`'s memory is fixed, not proportional to the read count** — verified rather than
assumed, because it is the one thing that could have sunk this route on a 32 GB machine: 8 M records
peaked at 13.2 GB and 32 M records at 12.2 GB. It is dominated by the reference GBZ, so 588 M records will
also sit near 12–13 GB.

**Query cost at real scale is acceptable.** A 1024-node query against the real 6.27 GB GBZ-Base plus a
4.17 GB GAF-Base took **155 ms**, in 31 MB of subprocess memory. That is a *pessimistic* figure: those
1024 nodes were scattered across the whole of chr20's block rather than forming a contiguous window, so
the query touched 20 alignment clusters where a real windowed fetch touches far fewer. chr20's block is
6.59 M node IDs, so at `--read-window 1024` that is ~6,400 windows, or roughly 15 min of query time as an
upper bound — immaterial next to a multi-hour evaluation, and reducible with a larger window.

#### Artifact size: two wrong estimates, and why sampling could not settle it

This was estimated twice and both estimates were wrong; the built artifact is **20.74 GB for
596,017,764 alignments — 36.5 bytes/record**, which is **3.2× smaller than GAM** (112 bytes/record,
~66 GB). Recording the error because the reason generalises.

- First estimate, ~27 GB, came from a 7.8× GAF-text-to-database ratio on a *toy* graph. Rejected because
  that graph had 3-digit node IDs, making its GAF text unusually bloated.
- Second estimate, ~77 GB, came from sorting an 8 M and then a 32 M record *sample* of the real file.
  Also wrong, and more instructively so.

**A sample cannot predict this compression ratio, because the compression depends on sort locality.**
GAF-Base compresses column-wise within blocks of ~1000 alignments. In a globally sorted file a block's
reads sit on the same handful of nodes, and the node columns compress hard. A sample drawn from a
genome-wide *unsorted* file, then sorted, still has reads scattered across all 24 chromosomes within
every block — so the ratio measured on it reflects almost none of the locality the real artifact has.
The lesson is narrow but worth keeping: **extrapolating a compression ratio from a subsample is invalid
whenever the compression exploits ordering**, and the error here was a factor of 3.6.

So gaf-base wins on disk as well as build time. The one real disk consideration is the *intermediate*:
sorted GAF is ~338 bytes/record, i.e. ~199 GB uncompressed. `gaf-base construct` reads sequentially, so
piping `gaf-base sort -o <fifo>` into `construct <fifo>` avoids writing it at all — verified, and used.

The one real disk consideration is the intermediate: sorted GAF is ~338 bytes/record, i.e. **~199 GB
uncompressed** for the whole genome, which is uncomfortable against 399 GB free alongside a 77 GB
database. `gaf-base construct` accepts gzipped input and `gaf-base sort` defaults to stdout, so pipe the
sort through `gzip` (~27 GB) rather than landing the plain file.

#### Two things this turned up that constrain other choices

**`gbz-base construct` could not find top-level chains for this graph: 2 of 46 components.** It warns
that "queries using `--snarls` or `--extend-snarls` may not work correctly". This does **not** affect the
read backend, which queries by node set (`-n`) and never asks for snarl extension. It does mean the
per-chromosome graph extraction in `call-per-contig.py` is **unavailable on this graph** without first
building a distance index and supplying `vg chains graph.gbz graph.dist > graph.chains` — which on 100 M
nodes is its own expense. Since §9.3 already chose `vg chunk` for the graph side, this costs us nothing
here, but it retires the gbz-base extraction route as an option for this dataset.

**`gbz-base query` errors out on a node ID that does not exist** — not a warning, the whole query fails.
Node IDs here are sparse (100.2 M nodes across a 224 M ID space), so this is easy to hit. The
`GafBaseSiteReadSource` implementation already filters each window to IDs for which
`graph.has_node()` is true, which is what makes windowed queries work at all; that filtering is
load-bearing rather than defensive, and this is the evidence.

### 9.8 Implementation log

Recorded as the plan was executed, because several steps turned out differently from the plan and the
differences are the useful part.

**Step 0, the gate: PASSED.** `##contig=<ID=chr20,length=66210255>` in the benchmark VCF matches the
graph's `CHM13#0#chr20` exactly. chr20 carries **163,602** small variants and **176,623** structural
variant records, and the small-variant benchmark BED covers **58.9 Mb across 647 intervals — 88.9% of the
chromosome**. So the confident region is substantial rather than a thin slice.

**Step 1, the chromosome cut: better than planned.** `vg chunk --gbz --contig chr20` produces a **GBZ**
component directly, in **64 s at 10.4 GB peak**, 72.9 MB output. This is preferable to the `-r` node-range
cut the plan suggested, because GBZ output **preserves the GBWT haplotypes** that `-z` enumeration needs —
where the GFA route of `call-per-contig.py` would have flattened them to `unknown#N#...`. Verified:
2,382,533 nodes (2.4% of the graph), `CHM13#0#chr20` at the right length, **4 haplotypes and 1 contig**
retained.

The cut also **confirmed the alt-node caveat from §9.7 empirically**: the component's node range is
`114,818,865 – 121,250,404`, whose maximum is **above** chr20's reference-path maximum of 121,243,142.
Filtering reads on the reference extent would have silently dropped reads on alt alleles. Read filtering
therefore uses the range reported by `vg stats -r` on the extracted component, not the reference path.

**Reference FASTA: taken from the graph, not downloaded.** aardvark needs one, and extracting
`CHM13#0#chr20` with `vg paths -F` (renamed to `chr20`) gives 66,210,255 bp — matching the benchmark
header exactly. Better than fetching CHM13v2.0 separately, because it is *provably* the sequence the calls
were made against rather than merely the same assembly name.

**Arm matrix correction: 4 of the 5 arms need the pack, not 3.** `poisson -z` fails with
`error[vg call] pack file (-k) is required`. The `-k`-optional change from stage 2 applies only to
`--read-likelihood`, because Poisson *genotyping* consumes support even when *enumeration* comes from
haplotypes. Only `readlik-z` is genuinely pack-free. That puts the pack — and hence the chr20 read
extraction it needs — on the critical path for everything except one arm.

**Snarl computation on one chromosome is cheap:** 42 s at 2.9 GB peak for chr20, which is the measurement
that retrospectively justifies §9.3's per-chromosome decision without ever having to test whether
genome-wide would fit.

**Read database build: the FIFO works.** `gaf-base construct` reads sequentially, so
`gaf-base sort -o <fifo>` piped into `construct <fifo>` avoids ever writing the ~199 GB uncompressed
sorted GAF. Confirmed on a 200 k-record sample before committing. `gaf-base sort`'s own temp files are
**zstd-compressed** (level 3, from `gaf_sort.rs`), so temp space is a fraction of the uncompressed size;
its *output* is not compressed, which is what makes the FIFO worth the trouble.

**The query target must be the whole-genome GBZ-Base, not a per-chromosome one.** Pointing
`gbz-base query` at a GBZ-Base built from the extracted chr20 component, while querying the
whole-genome GAF-Base, fails outright:

```
Error: The graph is not a valid reference for the alignments
```

So upstream *does* enforce the graph-consistency check that design §6.4 said we would have to police
ourselves — strictly, and as an error rather than a warning. Practically this means one 6.27 GB
GBZ-Base of the whole graph is shared by every per-chromosome run, alongside the per-chromosome GBZ that
`vg call` itself loads. It is a small cost, but it is not optional, and a smaller per-chromosome query
target is not available as an optimisation.

**The pipeline is proven end to end on real data.** A smoke run of `--read-likelihood --gaf-base` on the
chr20 component (using a 300 k-read subset) called 34,064 variants with a **96% window cache hit rate**
over 5,173 subprocess queries, and `aardvark compare` consumed the result against the real benchmark
without incident. The metrics from that run are meaningless — 300 k of 12.5 M reads — but every join in
the chain is verified: chr20 GBZ, whole-genome GAF-Base, whole-genome GBZ-Base, the reference FASTA
extracted from the graph, the benchmark VCF, and the benchmark BED.

One more plan step turned out to be unnecessary: **`vg call` already emits bare `chr20`**, with
`##contig=<ID=chr20,length=66210255>`, so the contig rename of step 6 is a no-op. It is retained as a
guard rather than removed, since its failure mode (zero true positives) is silent.

F1. On BASEPAIR it is much worse — precision 0.5757 against 0.7613, with 57,677 false-positive bases
against 23,018. GT credits a call whose genotype is right; BASEPAIR weights by bases. So the model is
finding insertion *loci* the Poisson caller misses, and getting the inserted *sequence or length* wrong
more often. **This is the most actionable follow-up** in the whole evaluation, and it is invisible if you
only look at GT.

Deletions go the other way: `readlik` wins on GT F1 (0.8228 vs 0.8148) *and* BASEPAIR F1 (0.8052 vs
0.7630), on better precision at slightly lower recall.

#### The 4-haplotype graph costs almost nothing — and the pack may be unnecessary

This was the headline risk in §9.2a: with only four haplotypes, `-z` enumeration could only propose alleles
present in those walks, so recall might be graph-bound rather than caller-bound. **It is not.** Comparing
across enumeration at fixed caller:

- `poisson` 0.9355 → `poisson-z` 0.9359
- `readlik` 0.9369 → `readlik-z` 0.9370

Haplotype enumeration is *marginally better*, not worse, and it is dramatically cheaper: `poisson-z` runs
in 74 s against `poisson`'s 152 s, and `readlik-z` in 434 s against `readlik`'s 679 s — while `readlik-z`
needs no pack file at all, removing a 37 s `vg pack` step and an 80 MB artefact.

The control was still worth running: without it, every number here would have carried an unresolved
"but the graph might be the limit" caveat. The answer is that on chr20 the two enumerations find
essentially the same allele set, so the ~7,500 remaining false negatives are shared and are *not*
explained by haplotype sampling. What does explain them is now an open question.

#### Cost

The read-likelihood model is **4.5–9× slower** than Poisson at matched enumeration (679 s vs 152 s;
434 s vs 74 s), for a ~0.1–0.3 point F1 gain. Peak RSS stays at 3.3–4.3 GB on a 32 GB machine, which is
the windowed read source working as intended — holding chr20's 13.3 M reads would have needed ~40 GB.

> **Superseded by §9.17.** The read fetching has since been made 5.8× faster, with byte-identical
> output: `readlik-z` now calls chr20 in **99 s against `poisson-z`'s 74 s** — 1.3×, not 5.9× — at
> 3.74 GB. The gap in this paragraph is a property of the old fetch path, not of the model.

### 9.10 Extracting a chromosome's reads: query the database, do not stream the GAF

The plan had step 2 stream the whole 28.8 GB GAF and keep records whose first path node
falls in the chromosome's block. That works, but it is the wrong tool once a GAF-Base exists, and the
difference is large:

| route | chr20 reads | wall |
|---|---|---|
| stream + filter the 28.8 GB GAF | — (abandoned) | **>90 min, unfinished** |
| query the GAF-Base by node set | **13,279,246** | **155 s** |

About **35× faster**, because the database is sorted by node ID and randomly accessible: chr20's reads
are fetched directly rather than found by decompressing the whole genome. 582 queries of 4096 nodes,
~0.25 s each. `vg pack` over the result then takes 37 s at 4.7 GB, giving an 80 MB pack.

This supersedes step 2 of §9.4 for any chromosome after the first, and it means the whole-genome
GAF-Base pays for itself twice: once as the read source for calling, and again as the fastest way to
materialise a chromosome's reads for anything else that needs them.

#### It also exposed a real bug in `GafBaseSiteReadSource`

Worth recording because the symptom was a number that looked merely surprising rather than wrong.

The first database-backed extraction reported a **~50% duplicate rate**. Chunked queries only overlap in
reads that straddle a chunk boundary, and with 4096-node chunks and reads spanning ~5 nodes the expected
rate is ~0.1%. Two orders of magnitude out is not a tuning issue.

The cause: **paired mates share a read name.** 20,000 records of this GAF carry 10,000 distinct names.
De-duplicating on the name alone therefore discards one mate of every pair. With the key corrected to
name-plus-start-position the rate falls to **0.15%**, matching the geometry.

The same flawed key was in shipped vg code — `GafBaseSiteReadSource::fetch_span`'s chunked path, whose
comment explicitly justified name-only de-duplication on the grounds that `skip_secondary` leaves one
alignment per name. True for secondaries, false for mates. Fixed in
[vgteam/vg#4990](https://github.com/vgteam/vg/pull/4990) (`2c89b28`) with a regression test. The path only
fires when a single site spans more than 4096 existing nodes, so it is rare — but when it fired it
silently halved the evidence at the largest sites, which is the worst place to lose reads.

Two lessons worth carrying: a de-duplication key is a modelling assumption and deserves the same scrutiny
as one, and **an implausible intermediate statistic is worth chasing even when the pipeline runs
cleanly** — nothing errored, and the only signal was a percentage that did not match the geometry.

### 9.11 Structural variants — assessed, and the picture reverses

> **Superseded by §9.15** in the same way: these SV numbers predate the floor change. With it,
> `readlik-z` reaches F1 0.5120 and edges past `poisson-z`'s 0.5104 rather than trailing it.

The first pass was **small-variant only**; the `stvar` benchmark was downloaded and subset but never
run. It has now been run for all five arms, reusing the same called VCFs (no re-calling: the callers emit
one VCF, and `smvar`/`stvar` are two truth sets over nearly the same confident region — 58.9 Mb against
59.4 Mb).

Of 176,623 chr20 `stvar` truth records only **2,052 are >=50 bp**; the rest is the local sequence context
an SV-aware haplotype comparison needs in order to place the SV. So only the `Sv*` categories say
anything about SV calling — the `ALL` row of the SV benchmark is dominated by the same small variants
scored in §9.9.

| arm | SV recall | truth TP | truth FN | SV calls | TP | FP | precision\* | **F1\*** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson` | 0.4667 | 784 | 896 | 713 | 336 | 377 | 0.4712 | 0.4689 |
| `poisson-z` | **0.5024** | 844 | 836 | 723 | 375 | 348 | **0.5187** | **0.5104** |
| `readlik` | 0.4536 | 762 | 918 | 750 | 348 | 402 | 0.4640 | 0.4587 |
| `readlik-nomismap` | 0.4446 | 747 | 933 | 799 | 352 | 447 | 0.4406 | 0.4426 |
| `readlik-z` | 0.4899 | 823 | 857 | 755 | 384 | 371 | 0.5086 | 0.4991 |

**On SVs the Poisson caller wins.** `poisson-z` leads at F1 0.5104 against `readlik-z`'s 0.4991, and
`poisson` beats `readlik` at matched enumeration (0.4689 vs 0.4587). That is the opposite ordering to
SNVs, where the read-likelihood model wins on both precision and recall. Both callers are also far
weaker here in absolute terms — **roughly half of true SVs are recovered at roughly half precision** —
which is worth stating plainly as a property of this pipeline on this graph, not of either genotyper.

This is consistent with the insertion finding in §9.9 rather than separate from it: the read-likelihood
model's weakness is in getting *sequence* right for long alleles, and SVs are the extreme of that. The
model finds SV loci at a comparable rate but produces more false positives (402 vs 377 for `readlik` vs
`poisson`), and the deficit widens with allele length.

**Enumeration matters far more for SVs than for small variants.** Haplotype enumeration beats support
enumeration by ~0.04 F1 for both callers (0.5104 vs 0.4689; 0.4991 vs 0.4587) — an order of magnitude
larger than the ~0.001 difference it made on small variants (§9.9). SVs are exactly where the
`FlowTraversalFinder` struggles to assemble a long allele from support, and where having the allele
already present as a haplotype walk pays. The four-haplotype graph is *helping* here, not limiting.

**The mismap term helps on SVs too** — `readlik` beats `readlik-nomismap` at 0.4587 vs 0.4426,
consistent with every other class.

#### A reporting defect in aardvark that had to be worked around

`aardvark`'s `summary.tsv` populates `truth_total` and `truth_tp` for the `Sv*` categories but leaves
`query_total`, `query_tp` and `query_fp` at **zero**, so its own `metric_precision` and `metric_f1`
columns come out as 0/0 for every SV row. Taken at face value that reads as "precision 0.0000", which is
not a result — it is a missing measurement, and it would have been easy to publish.

The per-variant decisions *are* present: aardvark's annotated query VCF carries a `BD` field
(`TP`/`FP`) on every query record, including the >=50 bp ones. Precision above is therefore recounted
from those, and F1 derived from it and the published recall. The recomputation is flagged in the results
table so no one mistakes it for aardvark's own output.

Note this also means the `--min-variant-gap 1000` SV preset from the GIAB README was applied as
recommended, and the failure is in reporting rather than in comparison.

### 9.12 Investigating the two deficits

Two separable deficits came out of §9.9/§9.11. They have different characters and, so far, different
levels of explanation.

#### Deficit A — insertion BASEPAIR precision. Insertion-specific, and *not* a scoring bias

At matched support enumeration, `readlik` against `poisson`:

| class | GT precision | GT recall | **BP precision** | BP recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SNV | +0.0026 | +0.0027 | +0.0020 | +0.0022 |
| **Insertion** | −0.0396 | +0.0238 | **−0.1856** | +0.0560 |
| Deletion | +0.0338 | −0.0174 | **+0.0949** | −0.0328 |

The first thing to note is that **it is not an "indel" deficit**: on deletions the read-likelihood model
is *better* on BASEPAIR precision by +0.095. Only insertions are bad. Both directions are the same
underlying preference, though — readlik favours **the allele with more sample sequence**: longer
insertions, and *smaller* deletions.

That preference is real and measurable in the calls. At the 105,175 positions where both callers make a
non-reference call, the called allele length differs at **703 (0.67%)**, and readlik picks the longer
allele **604 times against 99** — 6:1 — for **+60,024 bp** of extra called sequence, against an
insertion BASEPAIR false-positive excess of ~34,659 bp. So the whole of deficit A lives in well under
1% of sites; BASEPAIR weights by bases, so a few hundred large complex sites move an 18-point metric.

**Two mechanistic hypotheses were tested against controlled simulations, and both were refuted.**

*Hypothesis 1: the scorer favours longer alleles.* A single bubble with one insertion allele, reads
simulated from the reference so the insertion is always wrong:

| insertion | 10 | 20 | 50 | 100 | 150 | 200 | 400 bp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ln penalty | −20.7 | −34.6 | −76.1 | −145.2 | −214.4 | −283.6 | −560.2 |

Cleanly monotone at ~1.4 nats/bp — an affine gap behaving exactly as it should. **Longer wrong alleles
are penalised more, not less.**

Worth recording how this nearly went wrong. A first version put all seven insertion alleles in one graph
and read the penalties off the `--dump-likelihoods` columns assuming `allele_1..7` matched the order the
alleles were written. They did not — `vg call` emits them **longest-first**, so the table read backwards
and appeared to show a dramatic bias toward length. The one-allele-per-graph rerun produced identical
numbers in the opposite order. *Never infer an allele's identity from its column index in the dump.*

*Hypothesis 2: prefix-sharing alleles get a free pass.* `score_read_against_allele()` deliberately does
not charge for allele sequence falling before the first anchor or after the last, so a read ending inside
a long insertion should not pay for the tail it never saw. Tested with two alleles sharing a 30 bp
prefix, the long one extending to 400 bp, reads simulated from the short haplotype: the true 30 bp allele
scored 1.0 and the prefix-sharing 400 bp allele **−518.7**. Correctly rejected. In a simple bubble the
extra sequence always falls *between* two anchors and is charged in full; the free-pass geometry does not
arise.

**So the deficit does not reproduce in simple simulated bubbles, and the scoring model is sound on
everything constructed so far.** That is a useful negative result: it means the cause needs real
structural complexity — nested snarls, tandem repeats, many-allele sites — which is where the GIAB
README also puts its own known errors.

#### Mechanism, found: minority-allele contamination against the mismapping floor

Stratifying the 703 sites gives the first real signal. They are **multi-allelic** — median 2 ALT alleles
against 1 genome-wide, median 3 traversals against 2 — and **2.7× enriched for tandem repeats**: 31.8% of
the insertions called there are >80% explained by a motif of <=6 bp, against 11.7% across all calls. That
is exactly the class the GIAB README flags as its own known-error region.

Tandem repeats are also where mismapping produces a *minority* allele signal: reads from a longer repeat
copy elsewhere land on the expansion allele. That turns out to be the mechanism, and it reproduces in a
two-allele simulation. Truth is homozygous for a 30 bp insertion; a fraction of reads are contaminated
from a prefix-sharing 400 bp allele:

| contamination | 5% | 10% | 15% | 20% | 30% | 40% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson` | 1/1 ✓ | 1/1 ✓ | — | 1/1 ✓ | — | **1/2 ✗** |
| `readlik` | 1/1 ✓ | 1/1 ✓ | 1/1 ✓ | **1/2 ✗** | 1/2 ✗ | 1/2 ✗ |
| `readlik --no-mismap-term` | **1/2 ✗** | 1/2 ✗ | 1/2 ✗ | 1/2 ✗ | 1/2 ✗ | 1/2 ✗ |

**The read-likelihood model tolerates half the contamination the Poisson caller does — and the
mismapping term is the entire reason it tolerates any.**

The cause is structural, not a coding error. The model must explain *every* read. Under a homozygous
genotype a contaminating read can only be explained as a mismapping, at cost `ln(e_r)`; there is no
depth term to say "a het should be a 50/50 split" (design §2.4 makes that omission deliberate). Once
contamination exceeds roughly 15–20%, positing a second allele becomes cheaper than paying `ln(e_r)` for
each stray read — and because the stray reads at these sites carry the *longer* repeat allele, the second
allele posited is the longer one. That is the 6:1 length preference, arrived at without any length bias
in the scorer.

The Poisson caller is protected simply by comparing observed depth against expected: 20% is a poor fit to
a het and it says so. (Note `-b/--het-bias` is *not* the guard — that option belongs to the legacy `-B`
ratio caller and is rejected with the default caller.)

This also explains, retrospectively, why `--no-mismap-term` was worse on every class in §9.9: the term is
not a refinement, it is the model's only robustness mechanism.

**Where this lands: on a knob the design already identified.** The tolerance threshold is set by `e_max`
and by how much weight each read carries — exactly the calibration escape route of design §4.4 and
milestone M4/stage 4b. Two candidate fixes, both already planned rather than new:

- **Raise `e_max`**, buying tolerance directly. Cheap to test, but it weakens the term everywhere, not
  just at repeats.
- **Fit the `read_weight` discount** (stage 4b), so no individual read can force a genotype. This is the
  better-motivated fix, and this experiment gives it a concrete target: the crossover should sit well
  above the mismapping rate seen in tandem repeats.

A third, narrower option worth considering: the contamination that hurts is *allele-length-correlated*,
so a per-site guard requiring a minimum supporting fraction before admitting a substantially longer
allele would target the failure without touching the model globally. That is a heuristic and should be a
last resort, but it is the only one of the three that fixes insertions without also moving SNVs.

#### The deletion "advantage" is the same defect with a favourable sign

The one thing left dangling was why deletions ran the other way — BP precision **+0.095** for readlik
where insertions were −0.186. It looked like a second, opposing effect. It is not. It is the *same* bias,
and the metric flips sign underneath it.

Splitting the differing sites by direction, and asking only whether readlik's call carries **more sample
sequence** than the Poisson caller's:

| | sites | readlik has MORE sample sequence | LESS | net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| insertion-like | 501 | **451** | 50 | +16,776 bp |
| deletion-like | 411 | **341** | 70 | +55,788 bp |

Uniform, in both directions — 9:1 and 4.9:1 toward more sample sequence. A *single* bias then predicts
all four BASEPAIR movements, because BASEPAIR credit for a deletion is measured on bases the caller
asserts are **absent**:

- insertions: claim more inserted bases → recall **up** (+0.056), precision **down** (−0.186)
- deletions: claim fewer deleted bases → recall **down** (−0.033), precision **up** (+0.095)

So **the deletion result is not a win being threatened by a fix — it is the same over-calling of sample
sequence, wearing a favourable sign because claiming less is conservative in the deletion direction.**

That said, the effect is not *purely* a metric artefact: on `GT`, which does not weight by bases, readlik
is genuinely better on deletions (F1 0.8228 vs 0.8148) and marginally worse on insertions (0.7783 vs
0.7827). *(Both figures are at the old mismapping floor; §9.15 raises insertions to 0.8231, ahead of the
Poisson caller. The asymmetry analysed in this section is real but was largely a symptom of the veto
problem fixed there.)* The honest reading is that **the two callers are close on indels, and BASEPAIR magnifies the
difference in both directions** — spectacularly so for insertions. Quoting −0.186 as "the insertion
deficit" overstates it just as quoting +0.095 overstates the deletion advantage.

**Consequence for the fix.** A correction that removes the sample-sequence bias should improve insertion
BASEPAIR precision substantially and will cost some deletion BASEPAIR precision. Both should be judged on
`GT` F1 and on BASEPAIR *F1* rather than on precision alone, or the deletion movement will read as a
regression when it is the same correction working.

#### Deficit B — `-z` versus support enumeration. Fully explained, and not a genotyper effect

| class | poisson: support → `-z` | readlik: support → `-z` |
|---|---|---|
| SNV GT F1 | +0.0002 | +0.0000 |
| Insertion GT F1 | +0.0023 | −0.0000 |
| Deletion BP F1 | +0.0152 | −0.0003 |
| **SV insertion recall** | **+0.0386** | **+0.0411** |
| **SV deletion recall** | **+0.0329** | **+0.0317** |

The effect is **negligible below 50 bp and large above it**, and it is **the same size for both
callers** — +0.039/+0.033 for Poisson, +0.041/+0.032 for read-likelihood. That near-identity is the
finding: it is purely an *enumeration* effect with no interaction with the genotyper.

The interpretation is straightforward. `FlowTraversalFinder` assembles candidate alleles from support,
which works for a SNP or a short indel and degrades as the allele lengthens; a GBWT haplotype walk either
contains the long allele or does not. So for SVs, having the allele already written down as a haplotype
beats trying to reconstruct it — and the four-haplotype sampled graph is *helping* here rather than
limiting, the opposite of the §9.2a worry.

This also makes `readlik-z` the arm to prefer operationally: equal or better accuracy at every size
class, 36% faster than `readlik` (434 s against 679 s), and no pack file.

### 9.13 State of the investigation

**Settled.** One bias explains every indel and SV number in §9.9/§9.11: the read-likelihood caller
prefers the allele carrying **more sample sequence**, uniformly (9:1 at insertion-like sites, 4.9:1 at
deletion-like), and its cause is minority-allele contamination measured against the mismapping floor.
The caller must explain every read; under a homozygous genotype a stray read costs only `ln(e_r)`, and
past roughly 15–20% contamination positing a second allele becomes cheaper than paying that cost.
Because the strays at these sites carry the longer repeat copy, the allele posited is the longer one.
Reproduced in a two-allele simulation; the Poisson caller, which compares observed depth against
expected, tolerates ~40%.

**Settled: the `-z` difference is not a genotyper effect at all.** It is negligible below 50 bp, worth
+0.03–0.04 SV recall above it, and near-identical for both callers. `FlowTraversalFinder` degrades as
alleles lengthen; a haplotype walk either contains the long allele or does not.

**Four hypotheses tested and refuted.** Recorded because each was plausible and three of them would have
sent a fix in the wrong direction:

1. *The scorer favours long alleles.* No — the gap penalty is monotone at ~1.4 nats/bp in the correct
   direction. The first test appeared to show the opposite only because `vg call` emits alleles
   **longest-first** and the `--dump-likelihoods` columns were read in write order.
2. *Prefix-sharing alleles get a free pass on their unobserved tail.* No — a true 30 bp allele beat a
   prefix-sharing 400 bp one by −518.7. In a simple bubble the extra sequence always falls between two
   anchors and is charged in full.
3. *`-b/--het-bias` protects the Poisson caller.* No — that option belongs to the legacy `-B` ratio caller
   and is refused with the default caller. The protection is the depth model itself.
4. *Deletions are a separate, opposing effect.* No — same bias; BASEPAIR credit for a deletion is measured
   on bases asserted absent, so claiming less reads as better precision.

**Open, in priority order.**

1. **Calibrate the tolerance.** `e_max` and the `read_weight` discount (design §4.4, stage 4b / M4) are the
   levers, and this investigation supplies the target: the contamination crossover must sit well above the
   mismapping rate seen in tandem repeats. Judge on `GT` F1 and BASEPAIR **F1**, not precision alone —
   §9.12 explains why precision alone will misread the deletion movement as a regression.
2. **Why so few sites?** The bias fires at 703 of 105,175 shared calls (0.67%). If contamination past
   15–20% were common the count would be far higher, so something else gates it — presumably that only
   repeats generate enough correlated mismapping. Worth confirming, because it bounds how much a fix can
   recover.
3. **The `GQ` sweep** (M2) is now more interesting than before: the failing sites are 4× enriched for
   `GQ<=20` but their median `GQ` is 126, so a threshold trades recall for precision inefficiently. The
   sweep would quantify that trade rather than guess it.
4. **Mate correlation** remains unquantified (§9.6). Paired reads are treated as independent, and the
   contamination mechanism found here is precisely one where correlated evidence would inflate confidence.
   The two interact and should be looked at together.

**Not worth pursuing yet:** the SV deficit as a separate problem. It is the same bias at larger scale, and
the enumeration effect (+0.03–0.04 from `-z`) is larger than the caller difference (−0.011), so SV
accuracy on this graph is gated by traversal finding rather than genotyping.

### 9.14 Calibration: what the two knobs actually do

**Scope, stated first because it is the thing most likely to be misread: this is not a MAPQ→confidence
lookup table, and no large simulation is needed.** A per-MAPQ table would have ~61 bins, each needing
enough mismapping events to estimate a rate, which requires per-read origin truth in quantity. Design §8
decision 6 rejected that ("MAPQ at face value, no recalibration table") and it is not reopened here. What
is fitted is **two scalars**, and the objective is a *behaviour* — the contamination fraction at which the
caller stops recovering a known homozygote — which is measurable in a two-allele simulation that runs in
seconds.

Both are now implemented as opt-in flags, defaults unchanged (`vg test` unchanged at 207 + 39
assertions):

- `--read-weight W` — new. Counts each read as `W` independent observations:
  `total += W * log(...)` in `genotype_likelihood()`. Justified as an effective-sample-size correction:
  mates share a fragment and a mismapping fate, and reads piled on a repeat copy mismap together.
- `--mismap-max P` — exposes the existing `max_mismap` clamp, previously hardcoded at 0.1.

#### `read_weight` cannot fix the bias. It is a confidence knob only

This corrects §9.12 and §9.13, which listed `read_weight` as a candidate fix for the insertion deficit.
**It cannot be**, and the reason is elementary once stated: it is a uniform positive scalar on *every*
genotype's total log-likelihood, so `argmax` over genotypes is invariant to it. Measured:

| `--read-weight` | GT | GQ | GL |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 1/1 | 83 | −127.0, −47.8, −20.0 |
| 0.5 | 1/1 | 41 | −63.5, −23.9, −10.0 |
| 0.25 | 1/1 | 20 | −31.8, −11.9, −5.0 |

`GL` and `GQ` scale exactly linearly; the call never moves. So `read_weight` remains the right instrument
for **stage 4b's reliability curve** — GQ inflation is precisely what it fixes, and the model is
demonstrably overconfident because it sums correlated reads as independent — but it is the wrong
instrument for the length bias. Confirmed on the contamination test: `w` of 0.5, 0.25 and 0.1 all leave
the crossover exactly where `w = 1.0` leaves it.

#### `mismap-max` does change calls, with quick diminishing returns

Same contamination test (truth: homozygous 30 bp; contaminants carry a 400 bp prefix-sharing allele).
The Poisson caller's crossover is 40%.

| setting | 10% | 20% | 30% | 40% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline (`e_max` 0.1) | ok | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ |
| any `--read-weight` | ok | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ |
| `--mismap-max 0.2` | ok | **ok** | ✗ | ✗ |
| `--mismap-max 0.4` | ok | **ok** | ✗ | ✗ |

Raising the clamp from 0.1 to 0.2 moves the crossover from 20% to 30%, closing half the gap to the
Poisson caller. Going further to 0.4 buys nothing, which is worth knowing: the knob saturates, so this is
not a case where the deficit can simply be dialled away. That is consistent with the mechanism — beyond
some point the mixture term, not the mismapping floor, dominates.

#### Validated on chr20: neither knob fixes the deficit, and the reason is structural

The simulated gain does **not** transfer. Real chr20, `readlik-z`, small-variant benchmark:

All rows here are at the **old** mismapping *floor* of 1e-8; only the cap varies. §9.15 shows the floor
is the clamp that matters.

| setting | SNV GT F1 | Insertion BP prec | Deletion BP F1 | ALL BP F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline `e_max` 0.1 | 0.9759 | 0.5742 | 0.8049 | 0.8686 |
| `e_max` 0.2 | 0.9759 | 0.5700 | 0.8078 | 0.8674 |
| `e_max` 0.4 | 0.9758 | **0.5860** | 0.8074 | 0.8710 |

The largest movement is insertion BASEPAIR precision **+0.012** at `e_max = 0.4`, against the 18.6-point
gap to the Poisson caller. And the underlying bias is untouched:

| setting | sites differing | longer | net extra bp |
|---|---|---|---|
| `e_max` 0.1 | 786 | 622 | +55,115 |
| `e_max` 0.2 | 785 | 622 | +55,102 |
| `e_max` 0.4 | 790 | 623 | +55,853 |

**Why: the clamp is an upper bound on `e_r`, so it only binds for low-MAPQ reads, and there are almost
none.** MAPQ distribution of the 13.3 M chr20 reads:

| MAPQ | reads | share | effect of the clamp |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | 785,246 | 5.91% | binds |
| 2–9 | 53,344 | 0.40% | binds |
| 10–19 | 112,465 | 0.85% | inert |
| 20–59 | 368,383 | 2.77% | inert |
| **60** | **11,959,808** | **90.06%** | inert (`e_r ~ 1e-6`) |

The clamp can touch at most **6.3%** of reads. The evidence driving the false long-allele calls is
therefore **high-MAPQ**: those reads are not mismapped in any sense MAPQ can express, so the mismapping
term — the model's only robustness mechanism — is structurally unable to discount them however it is set.

#### Consequence: §9.12's mechanism is downgraded from "settled" to "partial"

The contamination experiment reproduced *a* failure with the right qualitative signature — a minority of
reads carrying a longer allele flipping a homozygous call — but it is **not the failure operating at the
real sites**. In the simulation the deciding reads were discountable; on chr20 they are MAPQ 60. The
claim that the deficit is "minority-allele contamination measured against the mismapping floor" is
therefore **wrong as stated for real data**, and is corrected here rather than in place, so the reasoning
that led to it stays visible.

What the evidence now says, more narrowly and more defensibly:

- The read-likelihood caller prefers alleles with more sample sequence, 6:1, at ~0.7% of sites
  (established, unchanged).
- Those sites are multi-allelic and tandem-repeat-enriched (established, unchanged).
- The reads driving those calls are **confidently mapped**, and the model is **confident** about the
  result — median `GQ` 126 at the differing sites. This is not a near-tie, so neither a tolerance-based
  tie-break nor a `GQ` filter would fix it.
- So either the scoring genuinely favours the longer allele on this evidence, or the reads genuinely do
  support it and the disagreement is with the benchmark. **Distinguishing those two is the next step**,
  and it needs per-site inspection of the likelihood matrix at real failing sites rather than any further
  global knob.

#### The knobs are still worth keeping

Neither is wasted, but their purposes are narrower than hoped:

- `--read-weight` is the right instrument for **stage 4b's reliability curve**, and only that. The model
  provably overstates confidence by summing correlated reads as independent, and this scales `GQ`
  linearly with no effect on any call — exactly what a calibration knob should do.
- `--mismap-max` is now measurable rather than hardcoded, which is how it was shown to be inert on this
  data. That is worth having even though the answer was negative: the previous state asserted 0.1 with no
  way to check it.

### 9.15 The fix: floor the per-read veto. Large, and on every class

The insight that unlocked this is not mine: **MAPQ measures confidence in a read's *location*, not in its
path through a particular site.** A read whose indel is placed a few bases off, or shifted within a
homopolymer, is still MAPQ 60 — so the mismapping term cannot touch it — yet it votes at full strength
for whichever allele its wrong path happens to match.

That reframes which clamp matters, and shows §9.14 swept the wrong one. The per-read term is
`log((1-e_r)·mixture + e_r)`, so a read fitting allele A perfectly and B not at all vetoes B by exactly
`ln(e_r)`. At MAPQ 60, `e_r ~ 1e-6` and **one read vetoes an allele by −13.8 nats**. The *cap*
(`--mismap-max`) bounds only reads whose `e_r` is already large — the 6.3% at MAPQ <= 9 — which is why
raising it was inert. The **floor** (`--mismap-min`, previously hardcoded at 1e-8) is what governs the
90% at MAPQ 60.

Read as "P(this read's evidence at this site is unreliable)", a floor of 1e-8 asserts that a MAPQ 60 read
is locally misaligned once in 10^8 sites, which nobody believes. Setting it to 0.01 caps a single read's
veto at −4.6 nats.

#### Result on real chr20, against the GIAB benchmark

| class | `poisson-z` | `readlik-z` floor 1e-8 | **floor 0.01** | floor 0.05 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SNV GT F1 | 0.9735 | 0.9759 | **0.9766** | 0.9745 |
| Insertion GT F1 | 0.7850 | 0.7783 | **0.8231** | 0.8346 |
| Deletion GT F1 | 0.8148 | 0.8231 | **0.8706** | 0.8840 |
| **ALL GT F1** | 0.9359 | 0.9370 | **0.9482** | 0.9495 |
| ALL BASEPAIR F1 | 0.8867 | 0.8686 | **0.8973** | 0.8954 |
| Insertion BP precision | 0.7613 | 0.5742 | **0.6226** | 0.6024 |
| SV F1 | 0.5104 | 0.4991 | **0.5120** | 0.5025 |

**With the floor at 0.01 the read-likelihood caller beats the Poisson caller on every class**, including
the two it previously lost — insertions (GT F1 0.7783 → 0.8231, from −0.007 behind to +0.038 ahead) and
SVs (0.4991 → 0.5120, from −0.011 behind to +0.002 ahead). Overall GT F1 rises +0.011 and BASEPAIR F1
+0.029, the latter turning a loss against Poisson into a win.

0.05 is better still on indel `GT` but costs SNVs (0.9766 → 0.9745) and is slightly worse on BASEPAIR and
SVs. **0.01 is the better default**; the optimum is somewhere between and is not worth over-fitting to
one chromosome.

#### The mechanism, corrected again

The bias metric that motivated the whole investigation barely moved: 786 → 757 differing sites,
+55,115 → +55,292 bp. So **the "sample-sequence bias at 0.7% of sites" was a symptom, not the driver**,
and §9.12's attribution of the deficit to those sites was wrong. Comparing genotypes directly shows what
actually changed — 1,493 genotypes (1.41%), plus 1,251 non-reference calls dropped entirely:

| transition | count |
|---|---|
| 1/0 → 1/1 | 614 |
| 0/1 → 1/1 | 606 |
| 1/2 → 1/1 | 184 |
| everything else | 89 |

**94% are het → hom.** The failure was spurious heterozygosity: a handful of locally misaligned reads,
each able to veto the homozygous hypothesis by −13.8 nats, forcing a second allele that is not there.
That is the contamination mechanism of §9.12 after all — but the contaminating reads are not
low-confidence and not discountable by MAPQ, which is exactly why every MAPQ-based lever failed and why
the floor works.

So the corrected chain is: **local misalignment (invisible to MAPQ) → a few reads with excessive veto
power → spurious het calls → over-called alt alleles, which show up as a length preference in the slice
that was first examined.** Four earlier hypotheses were refuted along the way (§9.12, §9.14); this one is
supported by a direct intervention that moves every metric in the predicted direction.

#### Caveats

Calibrated on **one chromosome of one sample against a draft benchmark**. 0.01 is a plausible local
misalignment rate for 151 bp Illumina reads in repeat-rich sequence, and it is certainly closer than
1e-8, but it has not been estimated independently — only chosen because it works here. Worth doing
properly: the rate is directly measurable from simulated reads with known origin, which is the one thing
tier 0 provides and tier 2 cannot.

### 9.16 The insertion BASEPAIR "residue" is a benchmark artefact

§9.15 left one deficit standing: after flooring the veto, insertion BASEPAIR precision was still 0.139
behind the Poisson caller (0.6226 vs 0.7613/0.7694), while insertion `GT` F1 was *ahead*. That
dissociation — more variants right, but worse per-base — was read as "something is still wrong with
insertion sequence." It is not. The metric is measuring something else.

#### Where the excess bases are

Called insertion bases against the `smvar` truth set, bucketed by allele length:

| arm | 1 bp | 2–5 | 6–15 | 16–49 | 50–199 | 200+ | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| truth | 4,400 | 10,128 | 12,332 | 12,094 | 0 | 0 | 38,954 |
| `poisson-z` | 4,352 | 9,899 | 10,960 | 8,377 | 2,910 | 3,370 | 39,868 |
| `readlik-z` | 4,455 | 10,788 | 13,087 | 10,947 | 4,321 | **69,851** | 113,449 |

Below 50 bp the two callers agree closely and `readlik-z` is nearer truth in every bucket. The whole
excess is the 200+ bucket, from **246 calls**, contributing **27,951 FP bases and zero TP bases**.

Note the truth row: **0 bases at 50 bp and above.** The `smvar` benchmark holds no record >=50 bp — that
size class lives in `stvar` — but the two confident regions overlap almost completely (58.9 Mb vs
59.4 Mb). A >=50 bp insertion called inside the small-variant confident region therefore has every base
scored FP, however right the call is. It cannot be scored correct.

#### The size-matched comparison

Restricting **both** callers to the range the benchmark can adjudicate — dropping any record with a
called allele >=50 bp from REF, applied identically to each (`scripts/tier2/size_matched.py`):

| arm | class | BP recall | BP precision | BP F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `sm50-poisson-z` | Insertion | 0.7637 | **0.8700** | 0.8134 |
| `sm50-readlik-z` | Insertion | **0.8578** | 0.8624 | **0.8601** |
| `sm50-poisson-z` | Deletion | 0.8628 | 0.8094 | 0.8353 |
| `sm50-readlik-z` | Deletion | 0.8603 | **0.8865** | **0.8732** |
| `sm50-poisson-z` | ALL | 0.8993 | 0.9385 | 0.9184 |
| `sm50-readlik-z` | ALL | **0.9238** | **0.9596** | **0.9413** |

The insertion precision gap collapses from **0.139 to 0.008**, and insertion BASEPAIR F1 flips from a
0.047 loss into a 0.047 win. **There is no insertion-sequence defect.** The unrestricted number was
measuring that one caller emits large insertions and the other does not — `poisson` and `poisson-z` emit
nothing at all at the two largest sites.

#### Are the large calls right?

`stvar` is what can answer that, and the answer is mixed but net positive: SV insertion recall 0.4976
(`readlik-z`) vs 0.4263 (`poisson-z`). Of the 246 calls with a >=200 bp insertion allele, **35 are
confirmed true**, **73 confirmed false**, and **138 fall outside the SV confident region** and cannot be
judged either way. Capping insertion length at 200 bp would buy +0.168 insertion BASEPAIR precision and
cost SV insertion recall 0.4976 → 0.4348 — it optimises one benchmark by discarding what the other
rewards, and is not a fix.

#### What *is* wrong: no depth-plausibility term

Among the 138 unjudged are calls that are plainly bad:

| position | called insertion | GT | DP | GQ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr20:25,849,044 | 61,958 bp | 1/1 | 7,873 | 256 |
| chr20:32,179,077 | 57,716 bp | 1/1 | 5,337 | 256 |
| chr20:1,629,728 | 33,050 bp | 1/1 | 291 | 256 |
| chr20:25,873,453 | 28,685 bp | 1/2 | 5,498 | 256 |
| chr20:25,792,993 | 23,450 bp | 1/1 | 932 | 256 |

Chromosome-median DP is 29; the Poisson caller's expected depth (`XD`) never exceeds 167 anywhere on
chr20. Median DP rises monotonically with called insertion length — 28 (1 bp), 28 (2–15), 28 (16–49),
35 (50–199), 62 (200–999), **330 (>=1 kb)** — so these are collapsed-repeat pile-ups in and around the
pericentromere, not haplotypes. They are `1/1` at saturated GQ because the reads really do align there.

The model cannot reject them, and the reason is structural rather than a tuning failure: it computes
P(reads | genotype) **conditioned on the reads it is handed**, and never asks whether that many reads
should be there. The Poisson caller gets this free — observed-versus-expected depth is its entire model,
which is why it declines these sites rather than genotyping them. Expected depth is already reachable
from `ReadLikelihoodSnarlCaller`, which subclasses `SupportBasedSnarlCaller` and holds a
`TraversalSupportFinder` for allele enumeration.

**Depth filtering is not the fix**, and the sweep says so directly:

| filter | records dropped | INS BP precision | ALL BP F1 | ALL GT F1 | SV ins recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `readlik-z` (none) | 0 | 0.6226 | 0.8973 | 0.9482 | 0.4976 |
| DP > 400 | 102 | 0.6227 | 0.8972 | 0.9481 | 0.4867 |
| DP > 200 | 195 | 0.6227 | 0.8973 | 0.9480 | 0.4783 |
| DP > 58 | 1,202 | 0.7092 | 0.9160 | 0.9477 | 0.4167 |
| length cap 200 bp | 246 | 0.7904 | 0.9303 | 0.9482 | 0.4348 |

Dropping above DP 200 removes every giant in the table above and moves insertion BASEPAIR precision by
**0.0001** — no benchmark charges for them. DP > 58 does help, but only as a blunt proxy for length, and
it costs real SVs. These calls should be fixed because they are wrong, not because they cost a score; a
depth-plausibility term in the model is the principled route, and a post-hoc depth filter is not.

#### Status

The insertion deficit that motivated §9.12 through §9.15 is now fully accounted for: a real defect
(over-strong per-read veto → spurious heterozygosity), fixed in §9.15, plus a benchmark artefact that
looked like a residue and was not. The remaining known-bad output is the impossible-depth giants, which
no metric on this chromosome scores.

### 9.17 Making the read path fast: 5.8× on chr20, byte-identical output

§9.9's cost table had the read-likelihood caller 4.5–9× slower than Poisson, which was the strongest
argument against using it. That gap was almost entirely the read fetch path, not the model.

HG002 chr20, HPRC v2.1 MC CHM13, 6 threads, GAF-Base backend:

| stage | wall | peak RSS | reads fetched | subprocess queries |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline | 570 s | 4.25 GB | 32.4 M | 4,842 |
| + node-ID bounds pre-filter | 564 s | 4.19 GB | 32.4 M | 4,847 |
| + move into cache, not copy | 487 s | 4.22 GB | 32.4 M | 4,856 |
| + window 4096 | 389 s | 4.66 GB | 32.7 M | 2,119 |
| + `posix_spawn` | 288 s | 4.86 GB | 32.7 M | 2,123 |
| **+ fetch by ranges, not span** | **99 s** | **3.74 GB** | **13.4 M** | **1,130** |
| *reference: `poisson-z`* | *74 s* | *2.9 GB* | — | — |

**Every row calls the same 105,936 variants, byte-identical.** Verified by MD5 of the VCF body at each
step, and again end-to-end: re-running all five arms through the harness moved only the cost table in
`docs/tier2-chr20-results.md`, with every accuracy figure identical.

| arm | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| `poisson` | 152 s | 156 s |
| `poisson-z` | 74 s | 72 s |
| `readlik` | 574 s | **115 s** |
| `readlik-nomismap` | 556 s | **115 s** |
| `readlik-z` | 506 s | **97 s** |

The Poisson arms reproduce within noise, which is what makes the read-likelihood rows comparable rather
than a story about a quieter machine. **`readlik` is now faster than `poisson`** at matched enumeration;
`readlik-z` is 1.35x `poisson-z` where it was 5.9x.

#### The measurement that framed everything

Running the same call with `--gaf-reads` — every read in memory, no windowing, no cache, no subprocess
— took **150 s at 18.2 GB**. That put a hard floor under what retrieval could be worth: everything
above 150 s was fetch, everything below was not. It also explained the baseline's shape, where **sys
was 448 s of 506 s** — an I/O and allocation profile, not a genotyper's.

The final 99 s is *below* that floor, because GAF-Base parses reads across six threads where
`--gaf-reads` parses 4.9 GB of GAF text on one.

#### Two of the four suspects were innocent

The counters said 610 M reads were considered to deliver 7.5 M — 1.2% selectivity — which looked like
the obvious target. Indexing that scan was worth **1%**. It matters only because it makes a bigger
window affordable, and the bigger window is worth 45%.

`gbz-base` itself was never slow: 0.04 s for a 380-node query, 0.22 s for 4,000 nodes, near-linear in
reads returned. The C-API letter's premise — that the subprocess is the compromise — stays refuted.

#### What it was

**Fetching by span instead of by ranges, and nothing suggested it in advance.** `for_each_read`
collapsed the caller's exact node ranges to `[min_id, max_id]` before fetching. For a site inside one
window that is exact; for a snarl sparse in ID space it is not. **215 sites — 0.16% of all sites —
spanned 13.2 M node IDs while wanting 133 k of them.** They accounted for most of the reads fetched
and half the subprocess queries. Fixing it dropped reads fetched from 32.7 M to 13.4 M against the
13.3 M actually on chr20: the 2.4× redundancy is gone, not reduced.

Found by adding two counters and looking, after the first three changes had between them recovered
less than half of what the in-memory floor said was available.

#### A false positive worth recording

`gbz-base query --reference-only` appeared to make queries **15× faster with identical read output**.
It does not: it panics with "Reference-only output is not supported for node-based queries" and writes
nothing, and the timing loop had been reading the previous run's output file. The lesson is the cheap
one — compare against a freshly deleted output, not a path that already exists — but it would have
been an easy result to publish.

#### Method

Each change was measured on the full chromosome before the next was written, and the VCF checked for
identity each time. Two of five hypotheses were wrong, and the largest effect was not on the original
list at all; that ordering is only visible if each step is measured separately rather than in a batch.

### 9.18 A 34-haplotype graph: recall up, precision down, and the callers separate

A second evaluation graph, `hprc-v2.1-mc-chm13-eval.HG002.hap32.gbz`, with **34 haplotypes**
(CHM13, GRCh38, and 32 recombinants) against the original's 4, plus its own alignments of the same
reads. HG002 is absent from both, so neither is circular. chr20, so every number is directly
comparable to §9.7–9.17.

Full tables in `docs/tier2-chr20-graph-comparison.md`.

#### The comparison is tight everywhere it can be, and honest where it cannot

| held fixed | verified how |
|---|---|
| reference sequence | `prep_hap32_chr20.sh` refuses to run unless the two graphs' `CHM13#0#chr20` paths are **byte-identical**. They are |
| truth VCFs and confident BEDs | reference-based, reused unchanged |
| read set | 13,278,140 chr20 reads against 13,279,246 — a difference of 0.008% |
| chromosome, sample, harness, vg build | unchanged |

**What could not be held fixed: the alignments.** Reads mapped to one graph cannot be scored against
the other, because the node ID spaces differ, so the 34-haplotype arm uses its own GAF. Graph and
alignment move together. That is what adopting a richer graph actually involves, but it means no
difference below is attributable to the graph alone.

The chr20 subgraph carries **2,781,046 nodes against 2,382,533** (+17%) and **34 haplotypes against 4**,
so the `-z` arms really do enumerate from 34 walks.

#### Every arm gets worse on F1; they do not get worse equally

| measure | `poisson-z` | `readlik-z` | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| small-variant ALL GT F1 | −0.0235 | **−0.0022** | 11x |
| size-matched ALL BASEPAIR F1 | −0.0260 | **−0.0060** | 4x |
| SV F1 | −0.0695 | **−0.0212** | 3x |

Recall rises for every arm and precision falls for every arm — `readlik-z` GT recall 0.9322 → 0.9466,
precision 0.9648 → 0.9453. More haplotypes offer more true alleles and more wrong ones. **The
read-likelihood model absorbs the extra ambiguity 3–11× better than the Poisson model on every axis**,
which is what scoring reads against individual alleles buys over aggregating depth.

#### SV recall is the one outright win, and only for the read-likelihood arms

| arm | SV recall | SV precision | SV F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson-z` | 0.5024 → 0.4887 (**−0.014**) | 0.5187 → 0.4017 | 0.5104 → 0.4409 |
| `readlik` | 0.4810 → 0.5101 (+0.029) | 0.4671 → 0.4101 | 0.4739 → 0.4547 |
| `readlik-z` | 0.5214 → **0.5726** (+0.051) | 0.5028 → 0.4294 | 0.5120 → 0.4908 |

SV insertion recall for `readlik-z` goes 0.4976 → **0.5580**. This is §9.11's finding sharpened: the
extra haplotypes supply better SV alleles, but only a caller that scores alleles individually can use
them — `poisson-z`'s recall *falls*. It still costs precision, so F1 declines: the operating point
moved, it did not simply improve.

#### The mismapping term does ~10x more work

`readlik` against `readlik-nomismap` is worth **+0.0066** ALL GT F1 here against **+0.0007** on the
4-haplotype graph, and suppresses **7,287** calls against **752**. More near-identical haplotypes means
more chances for a read to fit an allele it did not come from, which is exactly the failure §9.15
diagnosed. The floor holds under a graph that stresses it ten times harder — the best evidence yet that
0.01 was not merely fitted to one chromosome.

#### The BASEPAIR collapse is mostly §9.16 again

Unrestricted insertion BASEPAIR F1 falls ~0.13 for every arm, which looks alarming and mostly is not:
a richer graph calls more >=50 bp insertions, and the `smvar` truth set scores every base of those FP.
Size-matched to <50 bp, `readlik-z` insertion BASEPAIR F1 is **flat** (0.8601 → 0.8605) with recall up
0.036. Checking this was cheap only because `size_matched.py` already existed.

#### What this cannot settle

Some lost precision may not be error. A graph carrying 32 haplotypes will call real variation a *draft*
benchmark does not cover, and that scores FP. The size-matched insertion row — recall +0.037, precision
−0.034, F1 flat — fits "more truth and more noise in equal measure" and "more truth than the benchmark
knows about" equally well. Separating them needs a more complete truth set, not a different metric, and
nothing here should be read as showing the 34-haplotype graph is worse in absolute terms.

|---|---|---|

| `poisson-z` | 72 s | 106 s |
| `readlik` | 115 s | 134 s |
| `readlik-z` | 97 s | 116 s |

The Poisson arms roughly double; the read-likelihood arms rise ~20%. After §9.17 the read-likelihood

### 9.19 Plan: the `readlik-z` precision loss on the 34-haplotype graph

§9.18 reported `readlik-z` GT precision falling 0.9648 → 0.9453 while recall rose 0.9322 → 0.9466, and
read that as an operating-point shift. Scoping below shows that reading is **incomplete**: part of it is
a threshold effect, but a real loss of discrimination sits underneath. This section is the plan to find
it. Nothing here is implemented yet.

#### What scoping already establishes

**The loss is almost entirely spurious heterozygous SNVs.**

| class | 4-hap FP | 34-hap FP | Δ | precision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SNV** | 375 | **1,597** | **+1,222** | 0.9947 → 0.9776 |
| Deletion | 1,177 | 1,669 | +492 | 0.8866 → 0.8532 |
| Insertion | 1,563 | 1,622 | +59 | 0.8436 → **0.8522** |

Insertions *improve*. `query_fp_gt` — FPs that are the wrong genotype at a real variant — barely moves
for SNVs (110 → 127), so the extra 1,222 are calls at positions with **no truth variant at all**.

Of the **1,421** SNV FP positions new to the 34-haplotype run: **95% heterozygous** (1,343 `1/0`, 18
`0/1`, 54 `1/1`), **99.6% bi-allelic in the output**, DP median 40 (unremarkable), and GQ median 29 with
**52% at GQ <= 30**. That is the spurious-heterozygosity signature of §9.15, in a graph with eight times
the haplotypes.

**GQ ranks them correctly, and above GQ 50 the richer graph is the more precise one.**

| GQ >= | 4-hap precision | 34-hap precision |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.9648 | 0.9453 |
| 30 | 0.9793 | 0.9746 |
| 50 | 0.9841 | **0.9847** |
| 80 | 0.9875 | **0.9918** |

**But at matched precision the richer graph is worse through the operating band.** The PR curves cross
twice — 34-hap wins below 0.95 and above 0.985, loses in between:

| precision | 4-hap recall | 34-hap recall | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.9400 | 0.9365 | 0.9504 | +0.0139 |
| 0.9600 | 0.9365 | 0.9163 | **−0.0203** |
| 0.9700 | 0.9193 | 0.9053 | −0.0141 |
| 0.9800 | 0.8979 | 0.8844 | −0.0136 |
| 0.9850 | 0.8580 | 0.8629 | +0.0049 |

Max F1: 4-hap **0.9505**, 34-hap **0.9479**, both unfiltered. **So recalibration alone cannot recover
it.** Something costs ~2 points of recall at matched precision, and that is the target.

#### One mechanism ruled in by reading the code

`ReadLikelihoodSnarlCaller::genotype` takes the **argmax of the raw likelihood with no genotype prior**;
ties break to reference and nothing else discriminates. With `A` alleles there are `A(A+1)/2` genotype
hypotheses, so a richer graph does not merely offer more alleles — it offers a **larger set to maximise
over**, and the maximum of many noisy scores is biased upward. This is winner's curse, and it needs no
prior to arise. It predicts the observed signature directly: het genotypes outnumber hom ones
`A(A-1)/2` to `A`, so the bias falls disproportionately on heterozygous calls.

#### Hypotheses, cheapest decisive test first

**H1 — No prior against heterozygosity.** With no prior, a het need only beat hom-ref on likelihood, and
a couple of stray reads suffice. *Test, and this one needs no `vg` run at all:* the VCF carries `GL` for
every genotype, so apply a het penalty λ offline, recompute the argmax, and re-score with aardvark.
Sweep λ on both graphs. *Predicts:* a modest λ recovers precision at small recall cost, and the optimal
λ is **larger** for the 34-haplotype graph. If the 34-hap curve at its best λ meets or beats the 4-hap
curve at its best λ, the whole effect is a missing prior and the fix is one term.

**H2 — Winner's curse scales with the enumerated allele count.** *Test:* `--dump-likelihoods` already
emits the per-site read/allele matrix. Bin FP rate, GQ, and het fraction by the number of **enumerated**
alleles (not the 1–2 that survive into the VCF), for both graphs. *Predicts:* FP rate rises steeply with
allele count and the 34-hap allele-count distribution is shifted right; the two graphs should lie on
*one* curve when plotted against allele count. If they do, allele count is the whole story and the
remedy is a multiplicity correction, not a graph change.

**H3 — Local misalignment, amplified.** More near-identical haplotypes means more chances for a read to
fit an allele it did not come from — the §9.15 mechanism under greater stress, consistent with the
mismapping term already doing ~10x more work here. *Test:* `--mismap-min` sweep on the 34-haplotype
graph, reusing `floor_sweep.sh`. *Predicts:* the optimal floor is higher than 0.01. If so, **the floor
should scale with graph complexity rather than be a constant**, which is a concrete implementable
finding and would supersede §9.15's single value.

**H4 — The alignments changed, not the graph.** The two runs necessarily use different GAFs (§9.18).
*Test:* at the 1,421 FP positions, compare MAPQ distribution, depth and soft-clip rate between the two
alignment sets; and check whether those positions are depth outliers. *Predicts:* if mapping quality is
systematically lower there, part of the effect belongs to the mapper, not the caller. Cheap, and it
bounds how much of this the genotyper can own.

**H5 — Benchmark incompleteness.** A graph carrying 32 haplotypes will call real variation a *draft*
benchmark does not cover. *Test:* check whether each spurious ALT is carried by a recombinant haplotype
in the graph, and cross the positions against the benchmark's flagged difficult classes (homopolymers,
tandem repeats). *Predicts:* nothing actionable, but it bounds the achievable ceiling and stops us
tuning against benchmark error. Hardest to settle; do it last, and do not let it explain away H1–H3.

#### Ordering, and what would end the investigation

1. **H1** — hours, no calling runs. Decides whether a prior term is the whole fix.
2. **H2** — one dump run plus analysis. Decides whether allele multiplicity explains both graphs at once.
3. **H3** — a few calling runs at ~2 min each now that §9.17 landed.
4. **H4** — cheap, and reframes ownership if positive.
5. **H5** — last, and bounding only.

The investigation ends when the 34-haplotype PR curve either meets the 4-haplotype one through
0.95–0.98, or is shown to be limited by something outside the genotyper (H4/H5).

#### A reporting change this forces

§9.18 compared single operating points, which is what made the effect look like a clean
precision-for-recall trade. It is not: the curves cross twice. **Arm comparisons should report recall at
matched precision, not F1 at one threshold** — the outstanding M2 deliverable, now clearly necessary
rather than merely nice.

### 9.20 The precision loss was `--mismap-max`, not the graph

§9.19 planned five hypotheses. H3 (floor) and H4 (alignment) were run first. **H4 is confirmed and the
cause is a default parameter, not a property of the 34-haplotype graph.** H1, H2 and H5 were not needed.

#### The finding in one line

`--mismap-max 0.1` caps how unreliable a read is allowed to be. On the richer graph, **27.9% of reads at
the failing sites sit at MAPQ 0–1**, where the mapper is saying p(wrong) >= 0.79 and the cap tells the
model 0.1. Raising the cap to 0.5 removes **94% of the excess false-positive SNVs** and is **neutral on
the 4-haplotype graph**.

| config | GT rec | GT prec | GT F1 | SNV prec | SNV FP | INS F1 | DEL F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4-hap, cap 0.1 fl 0.01 *(default)* | 0.9322 | 0.9648 | 0.9482 | 0.9947 | 375 | 0.8231 | 0.8706 |
| 4-hap, **cap 0.5** fl 0.01 | 0.9315 | 0.9649 | 0.9479 | 0.9947 | 376 | 0.8236 | 0.8706 |
| 4-hap, cap 0.5 fl 0.05 | 0.9321 | 0.9668 | 0.9492 | 0.9925 | 524 | 0.8354 | 0.8841 |
| 34-hap, cap 0.1 fl 0.01 *(default)* | 0.9466 | 0.9453 | 0.9460 | 0.9776 | **1,597** | 0.8650 | 0.8733 |
| 34-hap, **cap 0.5** fl 0.01 | 0.9450 | 0.9592 | 0.9520 | **0.9937** | **443** | 0.8662 | 0.8743 |
| 34-hap, **cap 0.5 fl 0.05** | 0.9466 | **0.9679** | **0.9571** | 0.9921 | 550 | 0.8879 | 0.9067 |

**With both knobs set for the graph, the 34-haplotype graph beats the 4-haplotype graph's best
(0.9571 vs 0.9495) rather than trailing it.** §9.18's "richer graph trades precision for recall" was
measuring a mis-set parameter.

#### How it was localised

**The loss is spurious heterozygous SNVs.** SNV FPs 375 → 1,597 (+1,222) while *insertions improved*.
`query_fp_gt` barely moved (110 → 127), so these are calls where no truth variant exists. Of the 1,421
new FP positions: **95% heterozygous**, 99.6% bi-allelic, DP unremarkable, GQ median 29.

**Recalibration alone cannot fix it.** GQ ranks them correctly — above GQ 50 the richer graph is the
*more* precise one — but at matched precision it loses 1.4–2.5 points of recall through 0.95–0.98, and
the PR curves cross twice. Max F1 4-hap 0.9505, 34-hap 0.9479.

**Alignment is implicated, but not as "worse alignment".** Globally the two read sets are
indistinguishable (mean MAPQ 55.31 vs 54.65; the 34-hap alignments are *better* on identity and
divergence), and TP sites are identical between graphs. At FP sites:

| at FP sites | 4-hap | 34-hap |
|---|---|---|
| mean MAPQ | 48.35 | **39.79** |
| % MAPQ < 10 | 14.25 | **29.18** |
| % MAPQ = 0 | 5.82 | *4.54* |
| % MAPQ 1–9 | 8.43 | **24.64** |
| identity | 0.92104 | **0.96537** |

Genuinely unmappable reads get *rarer*; the entire growth is MAPQ 1–9, which triples. The richer graph
converts confident placements into **two-way ties between near-identical haplotypes** — reads that fit
well (identity 0.921 → 0.965) but cannot be placed. That is a different failure from the 4-haplotype
graph's, where FP-site reads simply matched badly.

**The cap then discards the mapper's warning.** At FP sites, MAPQ 1 alone is 23.3% of reads
(p(wrong) = 0.79, capped to 0.1 — understated 7.9x) and MAPQ 0 a further 4.5% (understated 10x).
Summed over the reads where the cap binds, the mapper's doubt is 4,953 expected-wrong reads; the model
sees 610. **The cap discards 8.1x of the mapper's stated uncertainty**, and the model, hearing
confident-looking support for a second allele, calls a het.

#### Two things the sweeps corrected

**H3's prediction was wrong, and so was a claim made mid-investigation.** The floor optimum does *not*
move with graph richness: **0.05 is optimal on both graphs**, with the same monotonic SNV degradation
above it. An earlier reading of "0.01 → 0.05" came from comparing against §9.15's two-point grid, where
0.05 already won on GT F1 and was rejected for costing SNVs and BASEPAIR. On a proper grid the curves
have the same shape.

**The two knobs are separable and pull in different directions.** The cap governs SNVs — it decides how
much a read's *placement* ambiguity counts. The floor governs indels — it decides how hard one read can
veto an allele — and it mildly *worsens* SNV precision (FPs rise monotonically with it on both graphs).
Reporting only aggregate F1 hides this: floor 0.05 improves overall F1 while making the very deficit
under investigation worse.

#### Why the default was wrong, and what to do

`--mismap-max 0.1` was set when the evaluation graph had 4 haplotypes, and §9.14 measured it as inert
*on that graph* — correctly, because only 6.3% of reads there sat at MAPQ <= 9. It is a value fitted to
the case where the population it governs barely exists. On a pangenome with realistic haplotype
diversity that population is a quarter of the evidence at the sites that matter.

The effect **saturates above 0.5** (cap 0.9 gives 0.9517 against 0.5's 0.9520), because beyond that the
cap only touches MAPQ 0–3, which is ~1,100 of 20,922 reads at these sites. So the exact value is not
delicate — a good property for a default.

**Done: the `--mismap-max` default is now 0.5** (vg `allele_likelihood.hpp`, `call_main.cpp`). Neutral
on the 4-haplotype graph (−0.0003 F1, one FP), worth +0.0060 GT F1 and 1,154 fewer false SNVs on the
34-haplotype graph. Verified end to end: calling with no `--mismap-max` flag now reproduces the swept
`--mismap-max 0.5` run **identically** on both graphs (CHROM/POS/REF/ALT/GT/DP/GQ).

The cap cannot simply be removed, and the reason is structural. At `e_r = 1` the per-read term becomes
`log((1-1)·mixture + 1) = 0` for every genotype, so the read contributes nothing to any of them and
silently disappears; many mappers also use MAPQ 0 to mean "multi-mapping" rather than literally
P(wrong) = 1. So the cap must stay strictly below 1. Read 0.5 as: believe the mapper, but never let one
read count for less than half a read.

Whether the **floor** default should also move from 0.01 to 0.05 is a separate decision with a real
trade: +0.0051 GT F1 on the 34-hap graph and +0.0013 on the 4-hap, against SNV precision 0.9937 → 0.9921
and 0.9947 → 0.9925. It is an indel-versus-SNV preference, not a bug fix, and is left open.

#### Still open

H1 (no genotype prior; the caller takes the argmax of the raw likelihood) and H2 (winner's curse over
`A(A+1)/2` genotype hypotheses) were never tested — the cap explained enough that they were not reached.
They remain the best explanation for the **residual** 443 FP SNVs against the 4-haplotype graph's 375,
and for the 95%-heterozygous signature, which the cap reduces but does not eliminate.

#### After the default change: what it fixed, and what it did not

Re-running every read-likelihood arm on both graphs under `--mismap-max 0.5`:

| arm | graph | GT F1 | SNV prec | SNV FP | SV F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `poisson-z` | 4-hap | 0.9359 | 0.9914 | 605 | 0.5104 |
| `poisson-z` | 34-hap | **0.9124** | 0.9583 | 3,036 | 0.4409 |
| `readlik-z` | 4-hap | 0.9479 | 0.9947 | 376 | 0.5164 |
| `readlik-z` | 34-hap | **0.9520** | 0.9937 | 443 | 0.4906 |

**The small-variant regression is fully reversed.** `readlik-z` on the 34-haplotype graph now beats
itself on the 4-haplotype graph (0.9520 vs 0.9479) while keeping the recall gain (0.9450 vs 0.9315).
More usefully, **the read-likelihood caller's margin over the Poisson caller widens from +0.0120 to
+0.0396 — 3.3x** — because the Poisson caller has no equivalent mechanism and loses 0.0235 on the same
graph. That is the strongest argument for the model so far, and the old default hid it completely.

`readlik-nomismap` is the control: it pins `e_r` to the floor so the cap cannot reach it, and its
variant count is **unchanged to the record** on both graphs. On the 34-haplotype graph it still carries
2,593 spurious SNVs against `readlik-z`'s 443. The mismapping term is what does the work.

**The cap did nothing for SVs.** `readlik-z` SV F1 on the 34-haplotype graph is 0.4906, against 0.4908
before the change and 0.5164 on the 4-haplotype graph — SV precision is still 0.4309 vs 0.5081. The
richer graph's SV *recall* advantage is real and large (0.5696 vs 0.5250) but it is bought with
precision, and none of that is the cap. **This is a separate open deficit**, not covered by §9.19's
hypotheses, which were framed from the SNV evidence. It deserves the same treatment: localise the FP
SVs, check whether they are enumeration artefacts, benchmark gaps, or a genuine scoring failure at long
alleles, before proposing a mechanism.

### 9.21 The floor default, re-measured at the corrected cap

§9.20 left the floor open as "an indel-versus-SNV preference, not a bug fix". Re-measuring it at
`--mismap-max 0.5` changes that reading twice over: **most of what the floor used to cost SNVs was the
old cap**, and **small variants and SVs peak at different floors**, so the default is a choice rather
than an optimum.

The earlier floor sweep is not evidence about this configuration. It was run at cap 0.1, the two clamps
interact, and carrying a conclusion across a configuration change is exactly the error that made 0.1 the
wrong cap. So the grid was re-run at cap 0.5 on both graphs, with 0.02 and 0.03 added to fill the
interior, **and scored on both benchmarks** — the first pass scored only small variants, which is how it
produced a recommendation that turned out to be dominated.

| floor | 4-hap small GT F1 | 4-hap SV F1 | 34-hap small GT F1 | 34-hap SV F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 *(old default)* | 0.9479 | **0.5164** | 0.9520 | 0.4906 |
| **0.02** *(new default)* | 0.9490 | 0.5145 | 0.9547 | **0.4912** |
| 0.03 | **0.9495** | 0.5121 | 0.9563 | 0.4860 |
| 0.05 | 0.9492 | 0.5074 | **0.9571** | 0.4789 |
| 0.10 | 0.9449 | 0.5033 | 0.9535 | 0.4619 |

**0.02 is chosen because on the 34-haplotype graph it is the only point that improves *both* metrics
against 0.01.** Everything above it buys small-variant accuracy by selling SV accuracy, at a worsening
rate. It is also the best point on an equal-weight sum of all four numbers.

**The choice is not forced, and the alternative is recorded rather than hidden.** Weighted by record
count the answer is 0.05: chr20 has 94,691 small-variant truth records against 1,680 SVs, so 0.05's
+0.0024 small-variant F1 is worth ~227 records while its −0.0123 SV F1 costs ~22. A caller run that only
cares about small variants should set `--mismap-min 0.05` explicitly.

The knob is indel-shaped. From 0.01 to 0.02 on the 34-haplotype graph, insertion GT F1 goes
0.8662 → 0.8764 and deletion 0.8743 → 0.8883, against SNV precision 0.9937 → 0.9934. Under the old cap
the same move cost roughly four times as much SNV precision, which is why §9.15 rejected it.

Verified end to end: calling with no clamp flags reproduces the swept configuration identically on both
graphs.

#### A process note worth keeping

The first pass at this recommended 0.05 on small-variant F1 alone, without running the SV comparison
across the floor grid. The SV cost — a consistent ~0.01 F1 on both graphs — only appeared when the full
arm set was re-run afterwards. **A default was changed on a partial metric.** The rule this suggests:
a sweep that will set a default has to be scored on every benchmark the project already runs, not just
the one that motivated the sweep.

#### Both defaults together

| | 4-hap small GT F1 | 4-hap SV F1 | 34-hap small GT F1 | 34-hap SV F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| old (cap 0.1, floor 0.01) | 0.9482 | 0.5164 | 0.9460 | 0.4906 |
| **new (cap 0.5, floor 0.02)** | **0.9490** | 0.5145 | **0.9547** | **0.4912** |

The 34-haplotype graph gains **+0.0087 small-variant GT F1 and +0.0006 SV F1**; the 4-haplotype graph
gains +0.0008 small-variant F1 for −0.0019 SV F1. Neither default was wrong in isolation on the graph it
was fitted to — both were wrong for a pangenome with realistic haplotype diversity, and each hid part of
the other's effect.

### 9.22 Plan: the BASEPAIR fall and the SV deficit are one thing, and the metric is suspect

Two apparent deficits on the 34-haplotype graph — BASEPAIR F1 falling across the board, and SV F1 falling
— turn out to be the same phenomenon, and the first job is to establish whether it is a *caller* problem
at all. Nothing here is implemented.

#### They are one phenomenon, and it lives entirely at >=50 bp

`readlik-z`, 4-hap → 34-hap, at the shipped defaults:

| measure | 4-hap | 34-hap | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| BASEPAIR ALL, all sizes | 0.9041 | 0.8460 | **−0.0581** |
| BASEPAIR Insertion, all sizes | 0.7406 | 0.6029 | **−0.1377** |
| **BASEPAIR ALL, size-matched <50 bp** | 0.9441 | 0.9428 | **−0.0012** |
| **BASEPAIR Insertion, <50 bp** | 0.8649 | 0.8688 | **+0.0039** |
| GT ALL | 0.9490 | 0.9547 | +0.0057 |

**Restricted to <50 bp the BASEPAIR fall vanishes and insertions improve.** So there is no general
sequence-accuracy regression; there is one effect, confined to large alleles, that shows up as a
BASEPAIR fall when large calls are scored against a small-variant truth set (§9.16) and as an SV deficit
when they are scored against the SV benchmark.

#### The SV metric does not mean what it has been used to mean

Reconciling aardvark's two sides for `readlik-z`:

| | 4-hap | 34-hap |
|---|---|---|
| truth SVs explained (truth-side TP) | 882 | **956** (+74) |
| >=50 bp query records emitted | 668 | 780 (+112) |
| …of which individually credited TP | 337 | 337 |

**The richer graph explains 74 more true SVs while its count of individually-credited >=50 bp records
does not move.** The extra truth it finds is being credited through query records that are not themselves
>=50 bp — the caller is representing some large events as combinations of smaller records, which is a
legitimate representation and which aardvark's haplotype comparison accepts.

The SV "precision" reported since §9.18 is recomputed from per-variant `BD` labels over >=50 bp query
records (necessary because aardvark zeroes its own query columns for `Sv*`). That metric charges a
>=50 bp record as FP whenever it is not *individually* credited — which is exactly what a decomposed
representation produces. **So "SV precision fell" and "the caller got worse at SVs" are different claims,
and the reports have been eliding them.** Truth-side recall, which is unambiguous, says the richer graph
is *better*: 0.5250 → 0.5690.

(The two TP counts being exactly 337 looked like a bug in the recomputation. It is not: the two sets
share only 267 of 336 distinct position/length pairs, so the equality is a coincidence. Worth recording
because it was checked rather than assumed.)

#### Hypotheses, cheapest first

**H1 — It is representation, not error.** *Test:* for each uncredited >=50 bp query record, ask whether a
truth SV overlaps its locus and whether that truth SV is already counted as explained. Partition the FPs
into "no truth event here" (real errors) and "truth event here, credited elsewhere" (representation).
*Predicts:* if the 34-hap graph's extra 112 FPs are mostly the latter, the SV deficit is largely
metric artefact and the remedy is a better metric, not a caller change. This has to come first: every
later hypothesis assumes the deficit is real.

**H2 — An independent SV metric disagrees.** `truvari` has been the outstanding M3 deliverable
throughout and this is what it is for: it matches SVs by reciprocal overlap and size similarity rather
than by exact haplotype reconstruction, so it is insensitive to precisely the representation choice H1
suspects. *Predicts:* if truvari shows the 34-hap graph flat or better on SV precision, H1 is confirmed
from the other direction.

**H3 — Allele multiplicity: the model picks a near-miss.** More haplotypes offer many similar long
alleles; the argmax may take one that is close but not right. *Test:* for genuinely-wrong >=50 bp calls
(as classified by H1), compute edit distance from the called allele to the nearest truth allele at that
locus. *Predicts:* a near-miss distribution (small distances) means allele *selection* among similar
candidates, not spurious detection — and points at the scoring, not the enumeration.

**H4 — Unanchored allele sequence is free.** `score_read_against_allele` charges every read base but
**not** allele sequence falling before the first or after the last anchor (design §5.2). For an allele
longer than a read this makes extra length cost nothing, and a graph with 32 recombinant haplotypes
offers far more long alleles for that to favour. *Test:* stratify wrong >=50 bp calls by whether the
called allele exceeds the read length, and compare the called-allele length distribution against truth.
*Predicts:* systematic over-length. This is a concrete, already-documented asymmetry in the scorer and
the most specific mechanism on the list.

**H5 — Winner's curse over a larger genotype set** (the old §9.19 H2, still untested). With `A` alleles
there are `A(A+1)/2` hypotheses and the argmax of many noisy scores is biased upward; long alleles have
more scoring variance, so the bias should fall hardest on them. *Test:* bin wrong-call rate by enumerated
allele count and by allele length, from `--dump-likelihoods`. *Predicts:* both graphs lie on one curve
against allele count — which would make this a multiplicity correction rather than a graph problem.

**H6 — No genotype prior** (the old §9.19 H1, still untested). Testable offline from the `GL` field with
no `vg` run. Kept last because the 95%-heterozygous signature it was framed for was a *small-variant*
observation, and the evidence here is about long alleles.

#### What would close it

Either H1/H2 show the deficit is mostly metric — in which case the deliverable is a corrected SV metric
and a retraction of the "SV precision fell" claim in §9.18 and §9.20 — or they do not, and H3/H4 identify
which of selection or scoring is at fault. **The floor's split behaviour belongs to this investigation
too**: small variants prefer a floor of 0.05 and SVs prefer 0.02 (§9.21), which is unexplained and is
the same >=50 bp boundary showing up in a third place.

#### A standing correction to how arms are compared

§9.18 onward have compared single operating points and have quoted SV precision from a metric that
cannot distinguish a wrong call from a differently-represented right one. Both should change: report
**recall at matched precision** (the outstanding M2 item), and report SV accuracy from a metric that is
representation-insensitive.

#### 9.22 H2 result: truvari confirms the precision deficit and refutes the recall gain

Truvari 5.4.0, `bench --sizemin 50 --pick ac`, records split with `bcftools norm -m-any` and re-sorted,
against the same `stvar` truth and confident region. Truvari matches by locus proximity, size similarity
and sequence similarity, so it is insensitive to whether a large event is emitted as one record or
several — which is precisely the representation choice §9.22 suspected.

| arm | graph | recall | precision | F1 | TP | FP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `readlik-z` | 4-hap | 0.4654 | **0.5007** | 0.4824 | 356 | 344 |
| `readlik-z` | 34-hap | 0.4719 | **0.4187** | 0.4437 | 361 | 490 |
| `poisson-z` | 4-hap | 0.4902 | 0.4959 | 0.4930 | 375 | 372 |
| `poisson-z` | 34-hap | 0.4824 | 0.4029 | 0.4391 | 369 | 535 |

**Precision: the two metrics agree, so the deficit is real.** Aardvark put `readlik-z` at
0.5081 → 0.4309 (−0.077); truvari gives 0.5007 → 0.4187 (−0.082). The false-positive counts nearly
coincide as well — 331 vs 344 on the 4-haplotype graph, 443 vs 490 on the 34-haplotype. Two methods with
entirely different matching criteria reaching the same number is about as strong as this evidence gets.

**Recall: they diverge, and here the representation effect is real.** Aardvark reports +0.044
(882 → 956 truth SVs explained); truvari reports **+0.0065**. The gap is the decomposed representations —
truth SVs that aardvark credits because the haplotype reconstruction works, but for which the caller
emitted no recognisable >=50 bp record.

**So §9.22's framing was half right and both halves matter.** The suggestion that the deficit might be
largely metric artefact is **refuted** for precision. The claim that truth-side recall "unambiguously"
shows the richer graph better is **withdrawn**: most of that gain is decomposition, not detection. On a
representation-insensitive metric the richer graph is worse on SVs for both callers, entirely through
precision, with recall roughly flat.

That kills H1 as an explanation and promotes the mechanisms. The remaining candidates are unchanged in
substance but now have a sharper target — **~150 extra false >=50 bp calls on the richer graph, at
roughly flat recall**:

- **H3, near-miss allele selection.** Edit distance from wrong calls to the nearest truth allele.
- **H4, unanchored allele sequence is free** in `score_read_against_allele`, which favours alleles longer
  than a read — and the richer graph offers far more of them.
- **H5, winner's curse** over `A(A+1)/2` hypotheses, worst where alleles are long and numerous.

H1's partition of uncredited records is still worth doing, but as characterisation of *which* calls are
wrong rather than as a test of whether they are.

### 9.23 H5 and H6 results: multiplicity locates the failure, but neither prior nor clamp fixes it

#### The FPs, characterised

Truvari's false >=50 bp calls for `readlik-z`, 4-hap → 34-hap: **+146, of which +98 are insertions and
+8 deletions**; true positives move +8. By size the extra insertions are spread across every class and
concentrated at 50–300 bp (50–99: 79→109, 100–299: 65→107, 300–999: 24→37, 1k–10k: 8→21), so this is not
a handful of pathological giants.

They are **not near-misses**: median distance to the nearest truth insertion is **2,943 bp** on the
34-haplotype graph against 503 bp on the 4-haplotype one, and the fraction within 100 bp falls from 30.9%
to 23.9%. They are **low-confidence**: median GQ 11 against 42 for true positives, 64.1% at GQ <= 20.
They are **barely longer** than true positives (median 132 bp against 120), which is only weak support for
H4 — that hypothesis predicts a marked length excess.

**Thresholding does not recover it.** A GQ sweep re-run through truvari shows the 34-haplotype SV curve
strictly below the 4-haplotype one: to reach the 4-hap graph's unfiltered precision of 0.5007 it needs
GQ >= ~10, at which recall is 0.3673 against 0.4654. A **~0.10–0.115 recall deficit at matched precision,
at every operating point.** Unlike the SNV case in §9.20, this is a loss of discrimination, not a
mis-set threshold.

#### H5: allele multiplicity locates the failure exactly

Per-site enumerated allele counts from `--dump-likelihoods`, joined to truvari's verdicts by snarl ID:

| enumerated alleles | 4-hap FP rate | 34-hap FP rate |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.380 (133 FP / 217 TP) | 0.436 (112 / 145) |
| 3–4 | **0.622** (211 / 128) | 0.469 (107 / 121) |
| 5–9 | *impossible* | **0.746** (132 / 45) |
| 10+ | *impossible* | **0.768** (139 / 42) |

The 4-haplotype graph caps at **4** alleles per site; the 34-haplotype graph reaches **34**, though 92%
of sites remain bi-allelic. **The entire false-positive excess sits in allele-count bins the 4-haplotype
graph cannot produce**: −21 FP at 2 alleles, −104 at 3–4, **+132 at 5–9, +139 at 10+**. At >= 5 alleles
the caller is wrong about **three of every four** SV calls.

**But the same-curve prediction fails.** At 3–4 alleles the *4-haplotype* graph is markedly worse (0.622
against 0.469), so allele count alone does not determine difficulty — a 3–4-allele site is that graph's
hardest case and merely mid-range for the richer one. H5 is confirmed as a **driver**, not as a complete
account, and the confound between allele count and site difficulty was flagged before the measurement
rather than after.

Discrimination degrades with allele count, and **only** with allele count:

| enumerated alleles | 4-hap GQ median | 34-hap GQ median | 34-hap GQ <= 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 96 | **96** | 7.1% |
| 3–4 | 64 | 56 | 18.2% |
| 5–9 | — | 38 | 21.4% |
| 10+ | — | **15** | 35.4% |

At bi-allelic sites the two graphs are **identical** (median GQ 96). The richer graph does not make simple
sites harder; it has far more hard ones.

#### H6: a genotype prior reallocates, it does not discriminate

`ReadLikelihoodSnarlCaller::genotype` applies no prior at all. A Hardy-Weinberg prior is the principled
multiplicity correction — spreading non-reference mass `q` across `A-1` alternates means each specific
non-reference genotype loses prior mass as `A` grows — and it is testable offline from `GL`.
`scripts/tier2/genotype_prior.py`, swept on the 34-haplotype graph:

| prior | small-variant GT F1 | SV recall | SV precision | SV F1 | SV FP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | 0.9547 | 0.4719 | 0.4187 | 0.4437 | 490 |
| q = 0.1 | **0.9555** | 0.4562 | 0.4346 | 0.4451 | 445 |
| q = 0.05 | 0.9555 | 0.4484 | 0.4371 | 0.4427 | 434 |
| q = 0.01 | 0.9548 | 0.4340 | 0.4523 | 0.4429 | 396 |
| q = 0.001 | 0.9522 | 0.4196 | 0.4738 | 0.4451 | 352 |

**SV F1 is flat at ~0.443–0.445 across three orders of magnitude of prior strength.** False SVs fall
490 → 352 and recall falls in lockstep. The prior moves along the existing PR curve without improving it,
and even at its strongest does not reach the 4-haplotype graph's operating point. Small-variant F1 gains
at most +0.0008.

*Verification:* with the prior set to zero the same code reproduces the caller's genotype on 106,162 of
106,172 records (99.991%); the ten disagreements are exact ties, where the caller's documented tie-break
to reference differs from scan order. So the sweep measures the prior, not an indexing error.

*Bound:* `GL` spans only the alleles that reached the record, so the >= 5-allele sites carrying the 76%
false rate are collapsed to two alleles here. This test cannot refute a prior implemented in the caller
over the enumerated set — but it gives no encouragement either.

#### Where that leaves the mechanism

Not a decision-rule problem. Only **30.0%** of false SV calls are near-ties (top-two gap < 1 log10)
against 15.6% of true ones, so 70% are made with real separation — the model is often *confidently*
wrong. And in **73.5%** of false calls the runner-up is another non-reference genotype, so the model is
confident an event exists and is choosing among alternatives, rather than hesitating between reference
and not.

Three interventions have now been tried against this class of failure. The **cap** fixed SNVs and did
nothing for SVs (§9.20). The **floor** trades small variants against SVs and cannot improve both
(§9.21). A **genotype prior** reallocates precision and recall without adding discrimination. That
pattern — every knob on the *decision* moves the operating point and none moves the curve — points at
the **evidence**, meaning the scoring, rather than at how the evidence is weighed.

**H4 is the remaining untested mechanism and is now the leading candidate.**
`score_read_against_allele` charges every read base but no allele sequence falling before a read's first
anchor or after its last (design §5.2). A read ending inside a long allele therefore contributes nothing
to distinguishing that allele's length beyond its reach — it discards exactly the signal that would
separate similar long alleles, which is what these sites need. The weak length signal (132 against
120 bp) argues it is not the whole story, but it is the only hypothesis left that would reduce
*discrimination* rather than shift the threshold, and it predicts the insertion/deletion asymmetry
directly: surplus insertion sequence beyond a read's reach is free, whereas a deletion is charged as an
internal gap whenever a read spans it.

### 9.24 H4 refuted: the scorer is not the problem, and the graph is not "worse"

#### Three synthetic tests, all negative

`scripts/tier2/h4_length_discrimination.py` and `h4b_similar_alleles.py`, on constructed
graphs with `vg sim` reads:

| test | setup | result |
|---|---|---|
| nested lengths | REF / 100 / 300 / 1000 bp insertions sharing prefixes | **all four recovered** |
| similar alleles | up to 16 alleles differing by 1 substitution, truth present, reads re-mapped with giraffe | **all correct** |
| false-positive mode | up to 16 similar insertion alleles, **truth = reference** | **zero false positives** |

So the free-unanchored-sequence rule is not a length bias, and the caller does not invent insertions when
the sample carries none — even when the graph offers sixteen near-identical ones. **H4 is refuted.**

One methodological note that changed the second test's meaning. `vg sim -a` emits each read already on its
true graph path, so the scorer is handed the answer and discrimination is trivial — the first version of
that test scored 64/64 with GQ pinned at 126, which was the tell. Round-tripping through `vg giraffe` so a
mapper has to choose restores the real problem, and then **GQ collapses from 214 to 12–30** as the alleles
become similar while accuracy still holds. That reproduces the confidence collapse seen on real data
(§9.23: GQ median 96 → 15 with allele count) in a setting where the calls are nonetheless right.

#### What the real failing sites actually look like

Mapping quality and snarl size at truvari's >=50 bp verdicts, `readlik-z`:

| | mean MAPQ | MAPQ < 10 | nodes per site |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-hap FP sites | 19.34 | 56.5% | ~123 |
| 4-hap TP sites | 47.00 | 15.2% | ~20 |
| 34-hap FP sites | **28.85** | **45.7%** | ~123 |
| 34-hap TP sites | 44.01 | 20.8% | ~20 |

False SV calls happen at **large, complex, badly-mapped snarls on both graphs** — six times the nodes of a
site that yields a true call. But the 34-haplotype graph's false-call sites are **better** mapped than the
4-haplotype graph's, not worse. The extra false calls are therefore not explained by degraded mapping,
which is consistent with the cap having fixed SNVs and done nothing for SVs (§9.20).

#### The conclusion this forces

Put beside §9.23's allele-count table, the picture is not "the richer graph made the caller worse". The
caller has a **high error rate at multi-allelic sites on both graphs** — 0.622 at 3–4 alleles on the
4-haplotype graph, against 0.746 and 0.768 at 5–9 and 10+ on the 34-haplotype one. What changed is the
*number* of such sites: the 4-haplotype graph caps at four alleles and simply cannot present the hard
cases, while the richer graph has thousands of them.

That reframes the deficit as an **exposure** effect rather than a regression, and it explains why every
intervention so far has failed in the same way. The cap, the floor and a genotype prior all adjust how
evidence is *weighed*; none of them changes the fact that a large complex snarl with many long similar
alleles and half its reads below MAPQ 10 does not contain enough information to choose correctly. The
synthetic tests confirm the scoring extracts what is there — they reach the right answer whenever the
information exists, and lose confidence, not accuracy, as it thins.

#### What is left, and what is not worth trying

**Not worth trying:** further tuning of the clamps or the prior. Three independent interventions have each
moved the operating point without moving the curve, and the synthetic tests show the scoring is not
discarding available signal.

**Worth trying, in order:**

1. **Decline to call rather than call badly.** At >= 5 enumerated alleles the caller is wrong three times in
   four. Emitting a no-call, or a hard-filtered record, at sites whose evidence cannot separate the alleles
   would trade recall for precision *deliberately* rather than through a clamp. GQ already ranks these
   correctly; what is missing is the decision to act on it. Note this does not improve the PR curve — it
   makes the operating point honest, which is a different and more defensible goal.
2. **Reduce the exposure.** Most of these sites are large snarls where the enumerated alleles are
   near-duplicates. Collapsing alleles that no read can distinguish — before genotyping, not after — would
   cut `A(A+1)/2` sharply and is the only route that could move the curve rather than the threshold.
3. **Longer reads.** The information genuinely is not in 151 bp reads at a 1 kb allele with 45% of its
   coverage below MAPQ 10. This is a limit of the data, and worth stating plainly rather than tuning
   against.

### 9.25 Coverage signals: real, and smaller than they look

The genotype likelihood uses only *which* alleles a read fits. Its mixture assumes each
haplotype in the genotype contributed exactly `1/|G|` of the reads — no sampling model, no
length adjustment — so several quantities the caller already computes never influence the
ranking of a call. `scripts/tier2/allele_support_table.py` recovers them from
`--dump-likelihoods`; `signal_value.py` asks whether any of them adds anything **beyond GQ**,
which is the only fair test since GQ is already emitted and already ranks well.

Held-out logistic fits, all scored calls, chr20:

| signal | AUC alone (34-hap) | added to GQ, 34-hap | added to GQ, 4-hap |
|---|---|---|---|
| GQ | 0.9214 | — (0.9217) | — (0.8104) |
| `best_ln` (absolute fit) | 0.7878 | **0.9395** | **0.8912** |
| **allele balance** | **0.7327** | **0.9303** | **0.8406** |
| called-genotype share | 0.5613 | 0.9220 | 0.8196 |
| depth | 0.6733 | 0.9217 | 0.8111 |
| all five | — | **0.9454** | **0.9080** |

With 2,910 false calls the standard error is ~0.005, so the +0.024 and +0.098 gains are real.
**Allele balance carries signal and `best_ln` carries more** — and `best_ln` is close to
orthogonal to GQ (r = +0.18), which is why it adds most: GQ measures *relative* separation
between genotypes, `best_ln` measures *absolute* fit to anything at all. It is the signal
§5.3.1 named as a realignment trigger and never tested. Depth and called-genotype share add
nothing once GQ is present.

#### But the practical gain is small, and the reason is headroom

Precision at matched recall on the held-out half:

| recall | 34-hap GQ | 34-hap all five | 4-hap GQ | 4-hap all five |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.80 | 0.9946 | 0.9962 | 0.9870 | 0.9917 |
| 0.90 | 0.9838 | 0.9853 | 0.9780 | 0.9785 |
| 0.92 | 0.9705 | 0.9699 | — | — |

**A +0.024 AUC gain buys under 0.005 of precision.** Small-variant precision is already
0.97–0.99, so a better ranking has almost nothing left to correct. AUC rewards improvements
throughout the ranking; precision at high recall depends only on the tail, and that is where
the operating point sits.

**The headroom is in SVs**, where precision is 0.42–0.50. Single-signal AUCs there are GQ
0.682, `best_ln` 0.667, share 0.658 — but chr20 yields only ~339 scored >=50 bp calls, and a
five-feature fit cannot be validated at that n (SE ~0.036). Establishing whether these
signals help where it matters needs more chromosomes, not more analysis of this one.

#### A methodological correction, and how it happened

An earlier pass in this session reported that **allele balance "does not separate at all"**.
That was wrong. It compared medians of heterozygous balance restricted to >=50 bp calls —
n = 339, and a median comparison, which is blind to distributional differences and had no
power besides. Over the full call set with a fitted weight, balance has AUC 0.733 alone and
adds +0.009 to +0.030 to GQ.

Two other combinations were tried and discarded before the fitted comparison: an equal-weight
rank sum, which gave *opposite* answers on two subsets of the same data because it dilutes a
strong signal with a weak one; and a `--min-svlen 50` run using aardvark's labels, which is
meaningless because >=50 bp calls are unscoreable against the small-variant truth set by
construction (§9.16) and it left 18 true positives. Neither is reported as a result.

#### What to do

1. **Emit `best_ln` and allele balance as FORMAT fields.** They cost nothing to compute — the
   caller already has both — and downstream filtering can use them immediately. This is the
   cheap, low-risk half, and it does not require deciding how to fold them into GQ.
2. **Validate on more chromosomes before changing GQ.** The small-variant payoff is ~0.005
   precision, which does not justify altering a published field. The SV payoff is unmeasured
   and is the only reason to do this at all.
3. **Do not add a depth term for ranking.** It contributes nothing beyond GQ here. Note this
   is a different question from §5.3.3's depth-*plausibility* guard, which is about rejecting
   impossible pile-ups rather than ranking ordinary calls, and remains open.

### 9.26 chr6 validation: the coverage hypothesis is refuted, and something else is there

§9.25 ended with an explicit instruction to itself — *validate on more chromosomes before
changing GQ* — because chr20 offered only ~370 scored SV calls and a five-feature fit cannot be
believed at that n. chr6 was chosen next: 1.9× the small-variant truth records (313,919 vs
163,602), 3× the scored calls, and the MHC, which is the densest polymorphism in the genome and
so the hardest case for a haplotype-enumerating caller.

Both graphs were prepared with `scripts/tier2/prep_contig.sh`, which extracts the reference FASTA
from *each* graph and refuses to continue if the two disagree — the two runs are scored against
byte-identical sequence. `coverage_model.py` replaces `signal_value.py`: it reads `AD` and `BL`
straight out of the VCF (§9.25 step 1 shipped them as FORMAT fields), so no likelihood dump is
needed, and it reports precision and surviving FP count at matched recall rather than AUC alone.

#### The headline accuracy numbers, chr6 beside chr20

| set | arm | GT F1 | BASEPAIR F1 | SV F1 (truvari) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr20 4-hap | poisson-z | 0.9359 | 0.8867 | 0.4930 |
| chr20 4-hap | readlik-z | **0.9490** | **0.9041** | 0.4824 |
| chr20 34-hap | poisson-z | 0.9124 | 0.7861 | 0.4391 |

| chr6 4-hap | poisson-z | 0.9466 | 0.9169 | 0.5478 |
| chr6 4-hap | readlik-z | **0.9586** | **0.9227** | 0.5349 |
| chr6 34-hap | poisson-z | 0.9318 | 0.8076 | 0.4881 |
| chr6 34-hap | readlik-z | **0.9616** | 0.8312 | 0.4724 |

chr6 reproduces chr20 in every direction: `readlik-z` beats `poisson-z` on GT and BASEPAIR on both
graphs; GT F1 *rises* on the richer graph while BASEPAIR and SV F1 *fall*; and the BASEPAIR fall is
entirely precision (chr6 `readlik-z` 0.9013 → 0.7349). Multi-allelic records go 2.36% → 3.38% on
chr6 and 2.23% → 3.45% on chr20, so the exposure effect §9.24 identified is the same size on both
chromosomes. The MHC did not make chr6 special.

#### Small variants: the signals replicate almost exactly

Held-out logistic fits, all aardvark-scored calls. FP counts are on the held-out half at matched
recall — the quantity a filter is actually judged on, which AUC hides when false calls are 3% of
the total.

| dataset | n (TP/FP) | GQ | +`best_ln` | all six | FP @ R0.90, GQ → all six |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr20 4-hap | 86,830 / 2,786 | 0.8104 | 0.8913 | **0.9344** | 559 → **265** |
| chr20 34-hap | 87,152 / 2,910 | 0.9217 | 0.9394 | **0.9567** | 248 → **140** |
| chr6 4-hap | 260,041 / 7,010 | 0.8156 | 0.8967 | **0.9401** | 1,354 → **571** |
| chr6 34-hap | 259,302 / 6,767 | 0.9181 | 0.9395 | **0.9569** | 618 → **306** |

chr6 lands within 0.006 AUC of chr20 in every cell, at three times the n. **The combined score
removes 44–58% of false small-variant calls at unchanged recall.** That is a much better result
than §9.25's "under 0.005 of precision", and the difference is presentation, not data: §9.25
quoted precision, which moves from 0.9859 to 0.9933 and looks like nothing, while the same change
halves the false calls.

#### The user's hypothesis, tested directly, is wrong in both halves

The proposal was a binomial model of coverage skew with a length-aware prior on expected reads per
allele. Both halves fail, and the second fails informatively.

**The length-aware prior is empirically false.** The geometric argument is compelling: with 151 bp
reads, an allele carrying a 120 bp insertion has almost no read that can span it and still anchor
on both sides, so `w_i = max(1, R - len_i - 2F)` and an even split is the wrong null.
`allele_balance_by_length.py` measures the thing the argument predicts, over true ref/het calls:

| ‖len ALT − REF‖ | n (chr20 34-hap) | mean ALT share | mean AD/DP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 45,564 | 0.499 | 1.002 |
| 4–7 | 1,539 | 0.502 | 0.958 |
| 16–31 | 277 | 0.499 | 0.938 |
| 32–63 (truvari) | 19 | 0.531 | 0.938 |
| 128–255 (truvari) | 9 | 0.529 | 0.859 |
| 256–1000 (truvari) | 19 | 0.605 | 0.834 |

**The ALT share is 0.50 in every length class from 0 bp to over 1 kb**, on both chromosomes and
under both benchmarks' labels. The geometry does not apply because the caller never required a read
to span an allele — a read covering one breakpoint still fits one allele better than the other.
Scored against the geometric prior, true het SV calls look impossible and the resulting statistic
ranks *worse than chance* (AUC 0.46). It was removed.

**The skew statistic itself, with the correct flat null, is the weakest of the five signals
everywhere**: +0.003 to +0.006 AUC over GQ on small variants, +0.003 on SVs. Allele balance is
real (§9.25 was right to retract calling it absent) but it is not where the value is.

**What the same measurement does show** is that `AD/DP` falls with allele length — 1.00 at 0 bp to
0.83 (chr20) and 0.69 (chr6 34-hap) at 256–1000 bp. At long-allele sites a third of the reads fit
some allele the caller did not call. That is goodness of fit, not skew, and it is the signal the
`share` feature captures.

#### Structural variants: depth is the best addition, and its sign is inverted

| dataset | n (TP/FP) | GQ | +skew | +`best_ln` | +share | +depth | all six |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr20 4-hap | 170 / 198 | 0.6422 | 0.6404 | 0.6848 | 0.6459 | 0.6637 | **0.7356** |
| chr20 34-hap | 169 / 255 | 0.7189 | 0.7129 | 0.5711 | **0.8138** | 0.7841 | 0.7537 |
| chr6 4-hap | 425 / 340 | 0.6328 | 0.6383 | 0.6258 | 0.6487 | 0.6778 | **0.7101** |
| chr6 34-hap | 402 / 562 | 0.6689 | 0.6723 | 0.4892 | 0.7229 | **0.7577** | 0.7447 |

Precision at matched recall on the 34-haplotype graph, where the headroom is:

| | chr6, R = 0.70 | chr6, R = 0.80 | chr20, R = 0.70 | chr20, R = 0.80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GQ | 0.5564 (118 FP) | 0.4829 (181 FP) | 0.5398 (52 FP) | 0.5036 (69 FP) |
| GQ + depth | **0.6325** (86 FP) | **0.5828** (121 FP) | 0.6354 (35 FP) | 0.5469 (58 FP) |
| GQ + share | 0.5850 (105 FP) | 0.5365 (146 FP) | **0.7093** (25 FP) | **0.6195** (43 FP) |

**Depth and share are the two useful SV signals; `best_ln` is not one of them** — it is the
strongest signal for small variants (AUC 0.79–0.84 alone) and worthless or harmful for SVs
(0.489 on chr6 34-hap, and it *lowers* held-out AUC from 0.7189 to 0.5711 on chr20 34-hap).
And depth's direction reverses between the two classes **within the same dataset**: on chr6 34-hap
`depth_ratio` has AUC 0.6507 against the small-variant labels — low depth means false — and 0.3721
against the SV labels, where *high* depth means false. A single linear depth term in GQ would
therefore help one class and damage the other.

#### §5.3.3's pile-up guard: the signature is confirmed, the remedy is not a filter

Median depth and explained-read fraction for SV calls:

| dataset | DP median TP | DP median FP | AD/DP median TP | AD/DP median FP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr6 4-hap | 41.0 | 38.0 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| chr6 34-hap | 41.0 | **53.5** | 1.000 | **0.629** |
| chr20 4-hap | 38.5 | 39.0 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| chr20 34-hap | 40.0 | **51.0** | 1.000 | **0.636** |

On the richer graph a false SV call sits at a site with ~30% *more* reads than a true one, and only
63% of them fit any called allele. On the 4-haplotype graph neither difference exists. This is
§5.3.3's collapsed-repeat pile-up, now with a reproducible two-chromosome signature, and it is the
opposite of the intuition the investigation started from — the problem is not too few reads, it is
too many, fitting nothing.

`pileup_guard.py` scores it as a rule: flag when `DP > 1.3 ×` local median **and** `sum(AD)/DP < 0.8`.

| dataset | benchmark | flagged | of which FP | precision before → after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chr6 34-hap | truvari | 389 | 71% | 0.4170 → **0.5061** |
| chr20 34-hap | truvari | 161 | 78% | 0.3986 → **0.5057** |
| chr6 34-hap | aardvark | 675 | 25% | 0.9746 → 0.9751 |
| chr20 34-hap | aardvark | 282 | 21% | 0.9677 → 0.9683 |
| chr6 4-hap | truvari | 71 | 48% | 0.5556 → 0.5591 |
| chr20 4-hap | truvari | 24 | 75% | 0.4620 → 0.4767 |

The rule is well targeted — it fires 5–16× more often on the rich graph, and costs 0.2% of
small-variant true positives — but **it is not better than the ranking already available.** It
reaches ~0.72 recall at precision 0.506 on chr6 34-hap; thresholding plain GQ to the same recall
gives ~0.54. The gain in the table above is a gain over *no* filter, not over GQ. What genuinely
beats GQ is feeding depth and share into the score (0.6325 at R0.70, against 0.5564).

So §5.3.3's diagnosis stands and its proposed remedy does not: the fix is a size-conditional term
in the ranking, not a hard depth filter — which is also what §5.3.3's own DP-200 and DP-58
experiments concluded, for a different reason.

#### What to do

1. **Do not implement a binomial coverage-skew term.** The length-aware prior is contradicted by
   direct measurement, and the flat-null skew statistic is the weakest of five signals on both
   chromosomes, both graphs, and both benchmarks. This closes the question §9.25 opened.
2. **`best_ln` and `AD` are already emitted** and are the right vehicle for now: a downstream
   filter using them removes 44–58% of false small-variant calls at unchanged recall, without
   touching a published field.
3. **Any depth term must be conditioned on called-allele size.** Its sign is opposite for small
   variants and SVs in the same dataset. This is the concrete obstacle that has to be solved
   before §5.3.3 can be implemented, and it was not visible from chr20 alone.
4. **`best_ln` must be conditioned on size too**, for the same reason in the other direction: it is
   the best small-variant signal and is worthless-to-harmful for SVs.
5. **The SV n is still the limit.** chr6 doubles it to ~965 labelled calls on the rich graph, which
   supports the single-feature conclusions above (SE ~0.018) but still not a six-feature fit. The
   feature-selection differences between chr6 and chr20 in the SV table — depth best on one, share
   best on the other — are within that noise and should not be read as structure.

### 9.27 Depth cuts, and the explained-share discount in GQ

Two follow-ups to §9.26, and they land in opposite places. `scripts/tier2/depth_filter_sweep.py`
and `share_gq.py` share a loader (`filter_lib.py`) so both see the same calls, labels and
local-depth baseline.

Both are scored with one test that §9.26 established and that the pile-up guard failed:
**a filter must beat lowering the GQ threshold to the same recall.** Any filter that discards
calls buys precision by giving up recall, and so does a GQ cut; the only question is which
buys more. The `GQ prec` column in the sweep output is that comparison.

Two loader bugs were fixed before any of these numbers were believed. A relative dataset path
produced four empty tables that printed cleanly as a result rather than erroring. And recall
was first computed as query-side true positives over the truth total, which is wrong for
truvari: it matches several base records to one query record when the caller emits an SV that
the benchmark decomposed — 792 base matches against 425 query records on chr6 4-hap — so every
filter looked nearly twice as damaging as it is.

#### Depth: no minimum, and a maximum only in one specific case

ΔF1 against no filter, cutting on DP over a rolling median of nearby calls:

| cut | chr6 4-hap sm/SV | chr6 34-hap sm/SV | chr20 4-hap sm/SV | chr20 34-hap sm/SV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| min 0.5× | −0.003 / −0.011 | −0.003 / −0.014 | −0.003 / −0.004 | −0.003 / −0.009 |
| min 0.2× | −0.000 / −0.000 | −0.000 / −0.001 | −0.000 / +0.000 | +0.000 / −0.000 |
| max 2× | −0.000 / −0.099 | −0.001 / −0.043 | −0.000 / −0.043 | −0.001 / −0.027 |
| max 5× | −0.000 / −0.016 | −0.000 / **+0.022** | −0.000 / −0.009 | −0.000 / **+0.016** |
| max 10× | −0.000 / −0.006 | −0.000 / **+0.023** | −0.000 / −0.004 | −0.000 / +0.009 |
| max 20× | −0.000 / +0.000 | −0.000 / +0.016 | −0.000 / +0.000 | −0.000 / +0.008 |

**A minimum depth is not worth having.** Every setting that removes a material number of calls
loses F1 on both benchmarks, and — the decisive point — GQ thresholding reaches *higher*
precision at the same recall in every cell (0.9816 against 0.9762 on chr6 34-hap small
variants; 0.4516 against 0.4145 on its SVs). Low depth already depresses GQ, because few reads
means a small likelihood gap. There is nothing left for a separate cut to add.

**A maximum survives, but only narrowly.** At 5× the local median it beats GQ thresholding at
matched recall on the SVs of both 34-haplotype runs — 0.4946 against 0.4653 on chr6, 0.4517
against 0.4274 on chr20, so about +0.025 precision. Everywhere else it is dominated: on both
4-haplotype runs GQ wins at every threshold in the grid, and on small variants GQ wins in every
cell of all four. Tight cuts are much worse than they look: at 2× the local median, GQ
thresholding beats the filter by 0.09 precision on chr6 4-hap SVs.

So a max-depth cut is a real but narrow tool — rich graph, structural calls, loose threshold —
and it is not a default. `DP` is emitted, so anyone who wants it has it. This also settles
§5.3.3's remaining question in the negative: the pile-ups are real (§9.26) but a depth
threshold is not how to remove them.

#### Explained share: this one goes into GQ

`share` was the only signal positive in all eight cells of §9.26, which makes it the one
candidate for changing the emitted quality rather than leaving it to a downstream filter. The
evidence there came from logistic fits with per-dataset weights, which cannot ship. So the
question was re-asked in shippable form: is there a **single fixed formula with no fitted
parameter** that improves the ranking everywhere? Four families were tried:

| form | small variants | SVs | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `GQ · share` | +0.005 to +0.008 | +0.004 to +0.031 | **all 8 cells up, and up at nearly every operating point** |
| `GQ · share²` | +0.008 to +0.012 | +0.006 to +0.046 | better AUC, worse at high recall in 3 cells |
| `GQ · share⁴` | +0.012 to +0.015 | +0.008 to +0.056 | better AUC still, worse at high recall in 3 cells |
| `min(GQ, −10log₁₀(1−share))` | **−0.009, −0.006** on chr20 | +0.021 to +0.087 | breaks small variants |

The phred form is the one with a probabilistic reading, and the arithmetic shows why it fails:
at DP 30 a single stray read gives share 0.967, which caps a perfectly good SNV at Q14.8. A
tolerance before the cap engages (`t = 0.05`) repairs the AUC but still loses at the operating
points — 1,065 surviving false calls against GQ's 847 on chr20 34-hap. **Linear was the only
form that never made any of the eight worse**, so linear is what shipped.

`vg call` now emits `GQ = round(GQ_ratio × share)` and `GQI` carrying the undiscounted value,
with `--no-share-quality` to restore the old behaviour exactly. Measured end to end on the
caller's own output — which uses the exact fractional support rather than the rounded `AD`
this analysis reconstructed from, and therefore does slightly better than the offline
prediction in every cell:

Eight of eight improve on AUC; fifteen of sixteen operating points improve or tie. The single
exception is +2 false calls out of 148, on the smallest set in the table (n = 368).

Unfiltered F1 is **unchanged** on every benchmark — 286,557 variants and SV F1 0.5349 on chr6
4-hap before and after — as it must be, since the discount rescales a quality and does not
change a genotype. The gain is entirely in the PR curve, which is what was asked for.

#### The cost, stated plainly

A discounted GQ is no longer the phred-scaled odds of the top two genotypes. It is a quality
score, not a calibrated posterior, and anything that assumes GQ is a posterior — an
HWE-style prior, a Bayesian merge across samples — must read `GQI` instead. That is why `GQI`
is emitted unconditionally, including when the discount is off and the two are equal: a
consumer should never have to know which mode produced a file to know what its GQ means.

The justification for spending that is the mechanism, not just the numbers. The likelihood
compares genotypes using only *which* alleles each read fits, so a read fitting an allele
outside the call contributes to every genotype's likelihood and cancels out of the comparison.
GQ is structurally blind to whether the called genotype explains the pile-up at all. Sites
where a third of the reads prefer an uncalled allele get the same GQ as sites where none do,
and §9.26 measured that exact population: on the rich graph, false SV calls have a median
`AD/DP` of 0.63 against 1.00 for true ones.

#### Still open

- Whether a size-conditional depth or `best_ln` term can be built, given the sign reversal
  §9.26 found. The share discount does not address it; it is a different signal.
- Calibration. If GQ is to stay interpretable as a probability, the right form is a
  recalibration of the whole quality against observed error rates, not a multiplicative
  discount. That is a larger piece of work and needs more than one sample to fit.

## Appendix C. What is left, and in what order

Written after the document set was rationalized, from the state as it actually is rather than from
the milestone table's original framing. Ordered by what unblocks what, not by interest.

### 1. Land the vg change

Three commits sit unpushed on `read-likelihood-genotyping` in the fork. Before they go anywhere:

- ~~**Fixing the pre-existing 43 failures is worth doing**~~ — **done, and it was suite-wide.**
  `18_vg_call.t` now passes 138/138. The root cause was BSD `wc -l` right-aligning its count in a
  fixed-width field where GNU `wc` does not, so `is $(... | wc -l) 3` compares `"       3"` against
  `"3"` — passing on Linux, failing on macOS. Ten sites already worked around it; **533 across 44
  files did not**. Measured FAIL → PASS on six files, no regression on three already passing; the
  remaining 36 are the same mechanical substitution and cannot change behaviour on Linux, where the
  padding is never present. Committed separately from the feature work, and worth upstreaming on its
  own.
- **Four real breakages were hiding behind those 43.** Adding `AD`, `BL` and `GQI` moved `GL` from
  FORMAT sub-field 3 to 5, and four assertions read fields positionally — one was comparing `BL`
  against a `GQ` threshold. All now address fields by name, and two assertions were added for the
  new behaviour: `GQ` must never exceed `GQI`, and `--no-share-quality` must make them equal. The
  lesson is the general one: **a test suite that fails 43 times by default is not a regression
  signal**, and the cost of leaving it that way is not hypothetical.
- **The `GQ` change needs the PR description to lead with its cost**, not bury it: `GQ` is no longer
  a calibrated posterior on the `--read-likelihood` path. `GQI` exists for anyone who needs the
  posterior, and `--no-share-quality` restores the old behaviour. A reviewer who discovers that in
  the diff rather than the description will reasonably object.
- The default path is untouched — the discount lives inside `ReadLikelihoodSnarlCaller`, which is
  only constructed under `--read-likelihood`.

### 2. Structural variants — the one real accuracy gap

`readlik-z` trails `poisson-z` on SV F1 on three of four datasets. What is already established:
it is not a metric artefact (§9.22), not scorer bias (§9.24), and not fixable by either clamp, a
genotype prior, or the scoring rule (§9.23–9.24). It is largely **exposure** — the richer graph
offers multi-allelic sites the sparse one cannot produce.

That points at enumeration and at site multiplicity, neither of which has been tried:

- **Collapse indistinguishable alleles before genotyping.** Where two enumerated traversals differ
  only outside the reads' span, they compete for the same reads and split the evidence. Merging them
  before scoring is strictly better-founded than scoring both and picking one.
- **Decline to call at extreme multiplicity.** §9.23 found GQ already collapses at high allele
  counts; an explicit no-call is more honest than a low-confidence guess, and costs recall the
  benchmark may not actually be crediting.
- **Enumeration itself.** SV accuracy on this graph is gated by traversal finding, not genotyping.
  This is the largest available lever and the furthest outside the current change's scope.

### 3. Close the loop on the two signals that do not transfer

Depth and `best_ln` each help one variant class and hurt the other, with the sign reversing inside a
single dataset (§9.26). A size-conditional term is the obvious answer and is **not** worth building
on current evidence: the SV half of the fit rests on ~370–965 labelled calls. Either get more SV
data first (§4 below) or leave both as emitted fields for downstream use, which is where they are
now. Recorded as a decision, not an omission.

### 4. Breadth, which is the binding constraint on everything above

Two chromosomes, one sample, one read type, 151 bp Illumina. Every default here was fitted on that.
Cheapest useful increments, in order:

1. **More chromosomes.** ~30 min each now that `prep_contig.sh` is generalised. Three or four more
   would roughly triple the SV n and let the size-conditional question above be settled rather than
   deferred.
2. **A second sample.** The clamp defaults have never been checked against a genome that is not
   HG002. This is the single biggest unquantified risk in the shipped defaults.
3. **Long reads.** The read-likelihood model should be *better* suited to them than to 151 bp
   Illumina, and the whole evaluation is silent on it. Also the one setting where the geometric
   length argument refuted in §9.26 might actually apply.

  now runs in ~30 minutes per contig, so the gap it filled has closed.
